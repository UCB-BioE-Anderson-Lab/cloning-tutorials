## Why this one must be reshot

Two things changed since 2022, per JCA:

1. **Heat shock now uses a dedicated instrument** — the EchoTherm IC22 dry bath — rather
   than whatever the 2022 video shows. The instrument has its own training page at
   `docs/trainings/echotherm.md` and its own QR code on the machine.
2. **Flames are no longer used in this workflow.** The old video's plating segment is now
   actively wrong to show students.

✅ **The page has already been updated.** `docs/wetlab/transformation.md` previously taught
a **metal spreader + flame sterilize** method as option 2; that section is gone. Plating is
now documented as glass beads only, with an explicit no-open-flames note and 70% ethanol
spray for surface decontamination. The page and the new video will agree.

✅ Also settled: flame sterilization **does** still exist in the lab, but it is
**supervisor-only and not done at the sterile benches**. These tutorials are written for
students without EH&S training — potentially freshmen — so they teach the technique students
will actually perform. Flames stay out of this video entirely.

## Why film this

Competent cells are the most fragile material students touch all semester, and every rule
about them is a **manual technique** rule: how hard you pipette, how long they sit warm,
whether you made bubbles. None of that survives translation to text.

## Shots

1. **What transformation is** (~30 s) — bacteria taking up DNA from their surroundings.
   *E. coli* has to be made competent. We are isolating one molecule from a mixture and
   amplifying it a billion-fold.

2. **Handling competent cells** (~90 s) — **film this before the procedure, not during it.**
   Four rules, each demonstrated:
   - **Keep them cold at all times.** Aliquot goes −80 → ice, and stays in ice between
     every step.
   - **Do not pipette vigorously.** Show the correct motion: stir gently with the tip, then
     up and down *once or twice*, slowly. Then show the wrong way and name the cost.
   - **Bubbles mean lysis.** Show a bubbly tube; say those cells are dying.
   - **Never re-freeze a thawed aliquot.** One extra freeze-thaw craters efficiency.

   Say that students do not make their own cells — batches are made once a year by TSS and
   stored at −80.

3. **The EchoTherm** (~45 s) — **new material, not in the old video.** Show it properly,
   because this is the instrument's first appearance in the series:
   - Toggle switch **at the back right corner**
   - Two independent plates: **front at 4 °C, back at 42 °C**
   - **Defaults are already programmed** — wait for the display to reach setpoint, don't
     reprogram it
   - If anyone does change a setpoint, it must be reset to 4/42 afterwards
   - Anyone with lab safety certification may use it; no extra sign-off

   Keep this segment self-contained enough to be **cut out and reused** as the EchoTherm
   equipment short for its QR code (see plan 13). Same footage, two destinations.

4. **The transformation** (~90 s) — cells + KCM + assembled DNA. Chill on the **front (4 °C)
   plate**, then heat shock on the **back (42 °C) plate for 90 seconds**. Show the timer.
   Say that 90 seconds means 90 seconds, then straight back to cold.

5. **No rescue step — and why** (~45 s) — worth the time. Kanamycin and chloramphenicol
   block translation immediately, so cells need an hour of outgrowth first. **Ampicillin
   kills only actively growing cells**, so cells recover on their own and plate directly.
   Then: we actually use **carbenicillin**, because ampicillin degrades when heated during
   plate pouring, and the same `bla` gene covers both.

6. **Plating at the sterile bench** (~2 min) — **fully reshot, and the biggest change from
   2022.** Film at one of the two sterile benches, in order:

   1. **Prep the bench** — spray **70% ethanol**, wipe down with paper towels. Show it.
      This replaces flaming as the sterility step and is the first thing on camera.
   2. **Label the plate bottom** before anything goes on it — label, date, name.
   3. **Pour the beads on first** — about 10, from the bottle with **autoclave tape** on it.
      Say the count doesn't matter much. *(Note the order: beads before cells. The old page
      had this backwards.)*
   4. **Add 50–200 µL of cell suspension.**
   5. **Cap and shake side to side.** ← **the money shot of this video.**
      **Film the wrong way first:** swirl the plate and show all the liquid collecting at
      the perimeter with the middle unspread. Then show the correct straight back-and-forth
      motion, with the beads visibly tracking across the whole surface. Say "back and forth,
      not around." This is a physical skill that text cannot convey and the single reason
      this segment needs film.
   6. **Uncap and pour the beads through a funnel** into the collection beaker.
      **Say clearly that beads are not trash** — they get washed and re-sterilized. Show the
      funnel and beaker, because "pour them in the discard jar" is exactly the mistake.
   7. **Recap, invert, incubator** — not a shaker.

   **Do not film a spreader, a burner, or a flame at any point.** There is no second plating
   method to mention. Flame sterilization is a supervisor technique done elsewhere; it has
   no place in a student-facing video.

7. **Labeling** (~30 s) — label, date, and name on the **plate bottom, not the lid**. Match
   the plate name to the lab sheet ID (`pP6-79`). Return used DNA tubes to their rack.

## Watch out

- Heat shock is 42 °C / 90 s. Both numbers on screen as text.
- Do not leave cells out of ice "just for the shot." Take a fresh aliquot rather than
  filming bad practice.
- Quiz question 2 asks which method gives the highest efficiency (electroporation). The
  video should still mention electroporation exists and is ~100× better, even though pP6
  uses heat shock.
