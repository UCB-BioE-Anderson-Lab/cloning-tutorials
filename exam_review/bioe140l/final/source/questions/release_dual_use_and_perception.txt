slug: evidence_for_environmental_survival
difficulty: easy
topic: Identifying evidence that addresses persistence
question: |
  An assessment must address whether an engineered strain could persist outside the laboratory. What evidence speaks to that?
choices:
  A: Its growth rate in rich medium at thirty-seven degrees
  B: The copy number of the plasmid it carries in culture
  C: The brightness of its reporter when the inducer is present
  D: Survival in environmental samples at ambient temperature
  E: Its transformation efficiency when made chemically competent
answer: D
explanation: |
  Persistence is a question about the conditions the organism would actually meet, so the informative experiment puts it into those conditions: soil or water from the site, at ambient temperature, with the nutrients available there, followed over time. Laboratory growth in rich medium at body temperature is the measurement that tells you least about this, since it is the condition the strain was domesticated for.

slug: why_horizontal_transfer_matters
difficulty: easy
topic: Understanding why gene exchange is assessed
question: |
  Why does an assessment ask whether a released organism could exchange genes with native species?
choices:
  A: Because transfer would dilute the construct and reduce performance
  B: Because native species cannot take up plasmid DNA at all
  C: Because recipients would become classifiable as select agents
  D: Because transfer would prevent the organism from being recovered
  E: Because an introduced gene could persist even if the host does not
answer: E
explanation: |
  The engineered strain may well die out, but a resistance marker or a metabolic function it carried can move into organisms that are already established and stay in the environment indefinitely. That makes the consequence outlast the organism, which is why transfer is assessed separately from survival, and it is part of why markers are chosen carefully for anything intended to leave containment.

slug: containment_strategies_for_release
difficulty: medium
topic: Recognizing biological containment approaches
question: |
  Which approach is a biological containment measure for an organism intended to leave the laboratory?
choices:
  A: Autoclaving the culture before it is taken to the field
  B: Requiring the operators to wear gloves while sampling
  C: Making the strain dependent on a compound not found in nature
  D: Obtaining an institutional authorization for the field work
  E: Keeping the organism in a sealed cartridge during sampling
answer: C
explanation: |
  Engineering a dependence on a synthetic nutrient, or a kill switch that fires outside defined conditions, builds the limit into the organism so that escape does not become establishment. A sealed cartridge is a genuine physical containment that may well be the right answer for a field device, but it is engineering rather than biological containment, and authorizations and gloves address other things entirely.

slug: what_dual_use_means
difficulty: medium
topic: Defining dual use research of concern
question: |
  What makes a piece of research dual use?
choices:
  A: It is funded by both civilian and military sources
  B: Its results are published in two different fields at once
  C: Its findings could enable harm as readily as benefit
  D: It uses organisms classified in two different risk groups
  E: It can be performed in either academic or industrial settings
answer: C
explanation: |
  The concern is about the knowledge rather than the funding or the venue: work done for an entirely legitimate reason may also tell someone how to make a pathogen more transmissible, more virulent, or harder to treat. That is why the question is asked of the findings and their publication rather than of the investigator's motives, and why it arises in ordinary laboratories.

slug: gene_drive_distinguishing_feature
difficulty: medium
topic: Understanding what makes a gene drive different
question: |
  What distinguishes a gene drive from an ordinary engineered trait in a wild population?
choices:
  A: It spreads through the population faster than inheritance allows
  B: It persists only as long as the released individuals survive
  C: It can be reversed by ceasing to release the organism
  D: It acts on somatic cells rather than on the germ line
  E: It requires continuous release to maintain its frequency
answer: A
explanation: |
  A drive biases its own inheritance, so it can increase in frequency even when it carries a fitness cost and spread from a small release through a whole interbreeding population. That is precisely what makes it powerful against a disease vector and what makes it hard to recall, since stopping releases does not stop the spread the way it would for an ordinary trait.

slug: perception_versus_assessed_risk
difficulty: hard
topic: Reasoning about public perception of risk
question: |
  Public concern about an engineered organism is far greater than the assessed risk. How should that be handled?
choices:
  A: As irrelevant, since the assessment is the technical answer
  B: As evidence the assessment has underestimated the hazard
  C: As a real constraint to be engaged, distinct from the hazard
  D: As a problem solved by withholding details of the work
  E: As grounds to proceed only once concern has subsided
answer: C
explanation: |
  Perception and assessed hazard are different quantities and both are real: the assessment tells you what the organism can do, and public acceptance determines whether the work can proceed at all and on what terms. Treating concern as ignorance to be overridden, or as a hazard signal in itself, both misread it, and withholding information reliably makes it worse.

question: |
  A whole-cell biosensor is used on samples brought to a sealed cartridge and autoclaved afterward. How does that change the assessment?
choices:
  A: It does not, since the organism has left the laboratory
  B: It becomes a question of containment in the field, not of release

slug: markers_in_a_released_organism
difficulty: hard
topic: Reasoning about marker choice for release
question: |
  Why would an antibiotic resistance marker be avoided in an organism intended for environmental use?
choices:
  A: Because resistance genes are unstable outside the laboratory
  B: Because the antibiotic cannot be supplied in the field
  C: Because the marker could transfer and persist in the environment
  D: Because resistance markers raise the strain's risk group
  E: Because markers interfere with the sensing function itself
answer: C
explanation: |
  A resistance gene is the clearest case of something whose consequences outlive the organism carrying it, since it can move into resident bacteria and remain in a setting where that resistance matters clinically. Selection in the field is a practical problem too, which is why auxotrophic or synthetic-dependency markers are preferred, but the transfer concern is the governing one.

slug: reversibility_of_a_release
difficulty: hard
topic: Reasoning about whether a release can be undone
question: |
  Why is reversibility a central question for a deliberate release?
choices:
  A: Because regulators require a disposal plan for all materials
  B: Because an established population cannot simply be recalled
  C: Because irreversible releases are prohibited under all frameworks
  D: Because reversibility determines the organism's risk group
  E: Because an unrecoverable organism cannot be studied further
answer: B
explanation: |
  Once an organism establishes and reproduces in the environment there is often no way to withdraw it, so the decision cannot be revisited in the light of what is learned afterward, which is the opposite of how laboratory work proceeds. That asymmetry is why the assessment is made so carefully beforehand and why built-in limits on persistence carry so much weight.

slug: publication_and_dual_use
difficulty: hard
topic: Reasoning about publishing dual use findings
question: |
  A result would help defend against a pathogen and also show how to make it more transmissible. What does the dual use framework ask?
choices:
  A: That the work be classified and withheld from publication entirely
  B: That the benefit and the misuse potential be weighed before publishing
  C: That the work be abandoned, since the risk cannot be eliminated
  D: That publication proceed, since open science resolves such tensions
  E: That the decision be left to the journal's editors alone
answer: B
explanation: |
  The framework asks for deliberate consideration rather than a fixed answer: what the result enables for defense, what it enables for harm, who already has it, and whether some of it can be communicated differently. Automatic publication and automatic suppression both skip that judgment, and the decision involves the investigators, the institution and the funder as well as the journal.

slug: which_concern_gates_a_release
difficulty: medium
topic: Telling the concern that gates a release from product concerns
question: |
  An engineered strain is proposed for treating mine tailings in open settling ponds. Which question must be answered before the deployment can be approved?
choices:
  A: Whether it degrades the contaminant faster than the current treatment
  B: Whether the operators can be trained to dose the ponds correctly
  C: Whether the treatment costs less per ton than the current process
  D: Whether the strain establishes in the ponds and spreads beyond them
  E: Whether the process can be protected as a trade secret
answer: D
explanation: |
  Four of these decide whether the process is worth adopting and one decides whether it is permitted at all. Open ponds put live cells in contact with groundwater and wildlife, so approval turns on whether the strain persists, multiplies and moves beyond the site. Rate, cost, training and commercial protection are all real questions that a deployment clearing every biosafety requirement would still have to answer, which is what makes them the wrong category here rather than merely less important.
