slug: why_linkage_is_needed
difficulty: easy
topic: Understanding why a pooled selection needs linkage
question: |
  Why must the molecule performing the activity stay physically associated with the DNA encoding it?
choices:
  A: Because the DNA is what carries out the activity selected
  B: Because unlinked DNA is degraded during the selection step
  C: Because a selection cannot run unless the gene is on a plasmid
  D: Because linkage raises the enrichment achieved per round
  E: Because you recover an object and must sequence it to name it
answer: E
explanation: |
  A selection hands back a captured or surviving sample, not a label. The only way to find out what you captured is to read the attached DNA, so that attachment has to survive the selection. Plasmids are one implementation of the requirement rather than the requirement itself, and linkage is a precondition rather than a performance measure, so it does not by itself change the enrichment.

slug: pIII_fusion_in_phage_display
difficulty: easy
topic: Identifying how phage display achieves linkage
question: |
  In M13 phage display, how is a displayed antibody fragment connected to its gene?
choices:
  A: The gene is crosslinked to the protein after assembly
  B: The fusion is on P3 and the phagemid is packaged inside
  C: The gene is displayed on the surface beside the protein
  D: The protein is secreted while the gene stays in the cell
  E: The fusion is on P8, the only coat protein permitting it
answer: B
explanation: |
  The particle is the link: the phagemid carrying the fusion gene is packaged into the very particle whose tip displays the fusion protein, so capturing a protein captures its gene. Secretion would break linkage entirely, which is the error worth spotting here. P8 is used for multivalent display of very short peptides rather than for a fragment this size, and no crosslinking step is involved.

slug: selex_linkage_is_the_molecule_itself
difficulty: medium
topic: Recognizing that SELEX needs no separate linkage
question: |
  Why does SELEX need no display system or fusion protein?
choices:
  A: Because aptamers are selected by growth rather than binding
  B: Because the target is immobilized rather than the library
  C: Because the binder is the nucleic acid, so both are one object
  D: Because reverse transcription recreates linkage after elution
  E: Because aptamer libraries are small enough to screen singly
answer: C
explanation: |
  The linkage problem arises because the activity sits in a protein while the information sits in DNA. In SELEX the binder is the DNA or RNA, so what you elute is directly amplifiable and sequenceable. Immobilizing the target is part of the setup but not why linkage is unnecessary, amplification works precisely because linkage was never broken, and these libraries reach about ten to the fifteenth.

slug: yeast_display_mechanism
difficulty: medium
topic: Identifying the tether in yeast surface display
question: |
  In yeast surface display, what puts the protein on the outside of the cell carrying its gene?
choices:
  A: Fusion to Aga2, held by disulfides to Aga1 in the wall
  B: Insertion into a loop of an outer membrane barrel protein
  C: Packaging into a secreted vesicle along with the plasmid
  D: Fusion to P3 followed by infection with a helper phage
  E: Covalent coupling to agarose beads treated with cyanogen bromide
answer: A
explanation: |
  Aga2 is exported and held to the wall-anchored Aga1 through disulfide bonds, carrying the fused protein with it, so the displayed protein and its plasmid stay in the same cell. The barrel loop describes bacterial display with OmpX or a circular permutant of it, the phage route is a different system entirely, and the bead chemistry is how a SELEX target is immobilized.

slug: display_capacity_differences
difficulty: medium
topic: Choosing a display system from size and library limits
question: |
  You need to display a full-length 400 amino acid protein at the largest library you can get. Which statement is correct?
choices:
  A: Bacterial display on OmpX handles full-length at ten to the eleventh
  B: Phage reaches about ten to the seventh and yeast ten to the eleventh
  C: Both systems are limited by oligo diversity, not transformation
  D: Yeast takes full-length but caps near ten to the seventh clones
  E: Neither can display this length, so SELEX is the only route
answer: D
explanation: |
  The two systems trade against each other: yeast tolerates full-length proteins but its transformation efficiency caps the library around ten to the seventh, while phage reaches about ten to the eleventh. Bacterial display reliably handles peptides up to roughly eighty amino acids and is unreliable beyond that, transformation rather than oligo diversity is the limiting step, and SELEX selects nucleic acids rather than proteins.

slug: bulk_assay_breaks_linkage
difficulty: medium
topic: Recognizing a proposal that breaks linkage
question: |
  A pooled library of secreted enzymes is grown in one flask and the supernatant assayed, keeping the pool if activity is high. What is wrong?
choices:
  A: Nothing, provided the activity assay is sensitive enough
  B: Secreted enzymes cannot be assayed outside the cell at all
  C: The pool must be sequenced before the assay, not after
  D: The flask would need to be grown under antibiotic selection
  E: The product diffuses away, so activity names no genotype
answer: E
explanation: |
  Once the product leaves the cell it mixes with everything else in the flask, so a high reading says only that the pool as a whole contains something active. This is the classic linkage failure, and the fixes are to confine the product with the cell, in a droplet or liposome, or to keep it on the surface. Sensitivity does not address the problem, and the sequencing and antibiotic objections concern unrelated steps.

slug: compartmentalization_restores_linkage
difficulty: hard
topic: Using compartmentalization to supply linkage
question: |
  Why does running a pooled assay inside water-in-oil droplets or liposomes rescue a diffusible product?
choices:
  A: Because the compartment raises each variant's expression level
  B: Because compartments allow libraries beyond transformation limits
  C: Because compartments remove the need to sequence the hits
  D: Because the compartment shields the DNA from nucleases
  E: Because each compartment holds one gene and its own product
answer: E
explanation: |
  A compartment is an artificial cell: it keeps one template and whatever that template produces in the same tiny volume, which restores the association the bulk flask destroyed, and sorting the compartments then sorts the genes. Expression levels and nuclease protection are incidental, hits still have to be sequenced, and escaping transformation limits is a separate benefit of cell-free formats rather than the linkage argument.

slug: complementation_linkage
difficulty: hard
topic: Explaining linkage in a complementation selection
question: |
  A linker library is grown in a chorismate mutase knockout on medium lacking aromatic amino acids. What provides the linkage?
choices:
  A: The enzyme is displayed on the cell surface during growth
  B: The enzyme is attached to its own gene during translation
  C: The knockout strain carries a barcode for each variant
  D: Each variant grows in a separate well, so none is needed
  E: The plasmid sits inside the cell whose survival depends on it
answer: E
explanation: |
  The cell is the compartment. A variant whose linker lets the enzyme fold into a working monomer makes aromatic amino acids and the cell divides, and the colony that results carries the plasmid you sequence. This is why in vivo selections need no display machinery at all. Surface display and translational attachment are mechanisms from other systems, no barcode is part of this design, and separate wells would contradict the pooled nature of the selection.

slug: inferring_genotype_from_expression
difficulty: hard
topic: Recognizing an invalid substitute for linkage
question: |
  A proposal identifies the best variant in a pooled protein library by finding which variants are most abundant. Why does that fail?
choices:
  A: Because expression cannot be measured in a pooled library
  B: Because expression is readable only by cycle sequencing
  C: Because expression differences are too small to detect by reads
  D: Because abundance and the selected activity are different properties
  E: It would work, given sufficient sequencing depth on the pool
answer: D
explanation: |
  Abundance tells you which variants are present in quantity, which may reflect how well they clone, fold, or tolerate being made rather than how well they perform. The lesson is the one the SELEX data makes: the most abundant sequences in a final pool were not the enriched ones. Pooled expression is read by counting reads, and more depth only measures the wrong quantity more precisely.

slug: linkage_for_a_small_molecule_product
difficulty: hard
topic: Choosing a linkage strategy for a diffusible product
question: |
  Enzyme variants make a diffusible small molecule and you want those making more of it. Which approach keeps linkage?
choices:
  A: Phage display of the enzyme, then panning against the product
  B: Assaying the pooled culture supernatant by mass spectrometry
  C: Immobilizing the product and passing the library over it
  D: Yeast display of the enzyme, reading the product in the medium
  E: A biosensor in the same cell driving a selectable marker
answer: E
explanation: |
  A biosensor keeps the readout inside the same cell as the gene, so a variant that makes more product makes its own cell survive and the plasmid is recovered from the colony. Panning and immobilized product both select for binding the product rather than for making it, the pooled supernatant is the bulk assay that destroys linkage, and displaying the enzyme links the enzyme to its gene while still reading the product in shared medium.
