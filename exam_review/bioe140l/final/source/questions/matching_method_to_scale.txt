slug: a_handful_of_variants
difficulty: easy
topic: Choosing a workflow for a few variants
question: |
  You have eight defined promoter variants to characterize individually. What fits?
choices:
  A: Build each, pick a colony of each, and assay them in tubes
  B: Build a pooled library and sort the brightest by cytometry
  C: Array a picked library into plates with a colony picker
  D: Run a selection on a resistance marker and sequence survivors
  E: Barcode each variant and count transcripts by sequencing
answer: A
explanation: |
  Eight is a number you can simply handle: build each on purpose, grow it, measure it, and keep it as a named strain. Everything heavier exists to cope with numbers you cannot handle individually, and applying a pooled method here means doing extra work to recover identities you already had.

slug: thousands_of_variants
difficulty: easy
topic: Choosing a workflow at the thousand scale
question: |
  You need a per-variant value for roughly three thousand clones. What fits?
choices:
  A: Picking and assaying each clone by hand in tubes
  B: A pooled growth selection, sequencing the survivors
  C: A colony picker arraying into plates, read on a plate reader
  D: Scraping the library and sorting the brightest fraction
  E: Cycle sequencing each clone to infer its activity
answer: C
explanation: |
  Three thousand is beyond what anyone wants to pipette by hand and well within what a picker and a few dozen plates handle, which is exactly the regime laboratory automation was built for. The pooled approaches would discard the per-variant values that were asked for, and sequencing reports identity rather than activity.

slug: recovering_many_good_variants
difficulty: easy
topic: Choosing a workflow when many winners are wanted
question: |
  From a library of about a million you want the best several hundred variants, not just the single best. What fits?
choices:
  A: Array the whole library into plates and rank every clone
  B: Pick several hundred colonies at random and rank those
  C: Run a growth selection and keep every surviving colony
  D: Sort the top fraction and sequence the sorted pool as a whole
  E: Inspect the plate by eye and pick the several hundred brightest
answer: D
explanation: |
  Wanting many winners rather than one changes what the readout must do: sorting the top fraction and sequencing it as a pool reports which variants are enriched among the best performers, which is a set rather than a champion. Arraying a million clones is out of reach, random picks sample a fraction of a percent, and a survival selection returns everything above a threshold without ranking it.

slug: method_heavier_than_the_problem
difficulty: medium
topic: Recognizing a method heavier than the question requires
question: |
  You need expression values for twelve named constructs. Why not barcode them and count transcripts by sequencing?
choices:
  A: Because barcodes cannot be designed for only twelve members
  B: Because twelve constructs cannot be pooled in one culture
  C: Because the method costs far more effort than reading twelve wells
  D: Because sequencing cannot resolve twelve barcodes reliably
  E: Because transcript counts do not report expression level
answer: C
explanation: |
  The pooled readout is a solution to not being able to measure members separately, and with twelve you can, in a single plate, for a fraction of the effort and cost. Choosing the heavier method is as much a mismatch as choosing one that cannot reach the scale, which is the symmetry worth noticing. Each of the technical objections offered is false.

slug: why_enrich_before_arraying
difficulty: medium
topic: Reasoning about enrichment before individual handling
question: |
  Why does a sort precede plating and rescreening rather than replacing it?
choices:
  A: Because sorting enriches a population without identifying clones
  B: Because a sorter cannot distinguish bright cells reliably
  C: Because sorted cells must be regrown before they can be read
  D: Because the sorter's gates must be calibrated on pure clones
  E: Because rescreening confirms the sorter's calibration was correct
answer: A
explanation: |
  A sort hands back an enriched population, not a list of winners, so you still have to isolate individuals from it and measure them to find the best one and to keep it as a strain. The two steps do different jobs: the sort makes the number tractable and the rescreen turns the population into identified clones with values attached.

slug: scale_and_the_screen_selection_choice
difficulty: hard
topic: Connecting scale to the screen or selection decision
question: |
  Which pairing of library size and approach is appropriate?
choices:
  A: Ten thousand members assayed individually in separate tubes
  B: Ten to the twelfth handled by arraying into microtiter plates
  C: Five hundred members handled by a pooled growth selection
  D: Ten to the ninth members handled by a pooled selection
  E: A million members assayed individually by hand over weeks
answer: D
explanation: |
  A library in the billions can only be handled as a population, which is what a selection does, so that pairing is the one that matches. Arraying cannot approach that scale, individual assays cannot reach ten thousand by hand or a million at all, and a pooled selection on five hundred members is heavier than the problem when a plate of picks would answer it.

slug: coverage_constrains_the_design
difficulty: hard
topic: Reconciling the design with what can be screened
question: |
  Your design has a diversity of twenty thousand and your screen reaches two thousand clones. What is the better response?
choices:
  A: Screen two thousand and report the best as the optimum
  B: Sequence the two thousand to infer the untested members
  C: Collect 4.6 times the diversity in transformants and rescreen
  D: Assume random sampling makes the two thousand representative
  E: Narrow the degenerate code so the design fits the screen
answer: E
explanation: |
  Only a tenth of the design can be looked at, so the honest options are to shrink the design until the screen covers it, using a weight matrix or fewer degenerate positions, or to switch to a selection that handles the whole population. Collecting more transformants fixes coverage of the library in the freezer rather than of the assay, and a sampled maximum is not the true one.

slug: identifying_the_binding_constraint
difficulty: hard
topic: Identifying which step limits an experiment
question: |
  A library of fifty thousand is built, fully covered in transformants, and assayed clone by clone at five hundred clones per week. What limits the experiment?
choices:
  A: The transformation, which bounds the variants in hand
  B: The sequencing depth needed to confirm the library
  C: The construction, which must be repeated for each variant
  D: The coverage, which is insufficient at this diversity
  E: The assay throughput, which would take two years to finish
answer: E
explanation: |
  Coverage is stated to be adequate and the library exists, so the library is not the problem; fifty thousand clones at five hundred a week is about two years, which is the constraint that actually bites. Recognizing which step is binding is what tells you whether to change the design, the assay or the approach, and here it points at replacing the clone-by-clone assay.

slug: pooled_readout_for_per_variant_values
difficulty: hard
topic: Choosing a pooled method that still returns per-variant values
question: |
  You need a value for each of fifty thousand promoter variants, which is too many to array. What approach returns per-variant values?
choices:
  A: A growth selection, reporting which variants survived the drug
  B: A sort, keeping the brightest fraction for further work
  C: Barcoded transcript counting, normalized against input DNA
  D: Cycle sequencing of a few hundred picked colonies
  E: A plate reader measurement of the pooled culture
answer: C
explanation: |
  A pooled measurement that counts a tag per variant gives a number for every member rather than a surviving subset, which is what distinguishes it from a selection or a sort. Dividing message counts by the barcode's share of the input DNA turns those counts into expression levels. Picking a few hundred samples under one percent of the library, and a bulk reading returns one number for everything.

slug: scale_decision_comes_first
difficulty: hard
topic: Placing the scale decision in the workflow
question: |
  At what point is the scale of the workflow decided, and by what?
choices:
  A: After transformation, by how many colonies were recovered
  B: After sequencing, by how well the library was represented
  C: After the first assays, by how variable the results proved
  D: At the readout stage, by which instrument is available
  E: At the design stage, by the diversity and the values required
answer: E
explanation: |
  Two things settle it and both are known before any DNA is made: how many members the design has, and whether you need a value for each one or only the best few. Those decide whether the work is a handful of tubes, an arrayed screen, a pooled counting experiment or a selection, and deciding afterward risks building a library you have no way to interrogate.
