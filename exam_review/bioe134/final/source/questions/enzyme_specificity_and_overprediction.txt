slug: reaction_operator_overprediction
difficulty: easy
topic: Why reaction operators overpredict
question: |
  Why can a reaction operator overpredict possible biochemical products?
choices:
  A: It may encode chemistry without enzyme-substrate specificity constraints.
  B: It stores only LCMS retention times and not molecular structures.
  C: It requires every substrate to be a native metabolite of E. coli.
  D: It prevents any product from being represented as a graph.
answer: A
explanation: |
  A reaction operator can describe a possible chemical transformation, but enzymes do not act on every molecule that matches the abstract chemistry. Active-site geometry, binding, cofactors, and expression can restrict what actually happens. The other choices misstate what reaction operators represent.

slug: observed_false_meaning
difficulty: medium
topic: Positive and negative enzyme observations
question: |
  In a biochemical Observation record, what does `isObserved: false` most directly indicate?
choices:
  A: The reaction was observed but with a low retention time.
  B: The enzyme sequence could not be stored in the database.
  C: The tested enzyme did not detectably catalyze that reaction.
  D: The substrate was automatically converted into a Markush form.
answer: C
explanation: |
  A negative observation records that a particular enzyme-substrate reaction was tested or inferred not to occur detectably under the relevant conditions. This helps define specificity. It is not a retention-time flag, database-storage failure, or Markush conversion.

  A reaction operator matches two substrates equally well at the graph-pattern level, but the enzyme accepts only one of them. What explanation best fits the enzyme-specificity limitation?

slug: pks_nrps_complication
difficulty: hard
topic: Megasynthases and reaction prediction
question: |
  Why are PKS and NRPS systems especially challenging for simple reaction-operator prediction?
choices:
  A: They are always single-substrate enzymes with one freely diffusing product.
  B: They cannot generate molecules that are represented as chemical graphs.
  C: They are assembly-line enzymes with multiple domains and tethered intermediates.
  D: They are robotic liquid handlers rather than biochemical systems.
answer: C
explanation: |
  PKS and NRPS systems are megasynthases: multi-domain assembly-line enzymes that process tethered intermediates. Their products depend on domain architecture, module order, substrate loading, and tailoring steps. A simple monofunctional reaction-operator view can miss these constraints.

slug: model_enzyme_function_reason
difficulty: hard
topic: Why model enzyme function
question: |
  Which use best motivates modeling enzyme function rather than relying only on reaction operators?
choices:
  A: To guarantee that every LCMS peak has a single correct database identity.
  B: To replace all wetlab testing with a deterministic final answer.
  C: To convert every molecule into an executable robot protocol.
  D: To predict reachable compounds, side products, and plausible enzyme-substrate matches.
answer: D
explanation: |
  Enzyme-function models help evaluate mechanistic plausibility, predict side products and by-products, identify reachable compounds, and interpret LCMS data. They improve prioritization but do not guarantee peak identities, eliminate wetlab testing, or create robot protocols.
