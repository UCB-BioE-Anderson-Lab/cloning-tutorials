Is it safe? Who says so?

The system that answers those two questions
is being rewritten this month.

What makes work “regulated”

Who is actually watching

  • Biohazards — EH&S, and the Committee for Laboratory & Environmental Biosafety (CLEB), which is Berkeley’s IBC
  • Animals — the Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUC)
  • Human subjects — the Committee for Protection of Human Subjects (CPHS)
  • Radiation, lasers, chemicals — EH&S again

Above all of them: the NIH, for any institution that takes NIH money — which is to say, this one.

How risk is actually assessed

  • Virulence, pathogenicity, infectious dose — Shigella takes about ten organisms
  • Environmental stability — spores
  • Route of spread and communicability — influenza
  • Quantity, concentration, volume — a flask is not a fermenter
  • Availability of a vaccine or a treatment — hepatitis B
  • Allergenicity, and who is in the room

agent × modification × manipulation — not a lookup

Risk groups: a property of the agent

  • RG1
    Not associated with disease in healthy adult humans lab E. coli, S. cerevisiae
  • RG2
    Associated with human disease that is rarely serious, and for which preventive or therapeutic interventions are often available Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus
  • RG3
    Serious or lethal disease, for which interventions may be available Mycobacterium tuberculosis
  • RG4
    Serious or lethal disease, for which interventions are not usually available Ebola virus

The scale is the WHO’s. It classifies organisms that have been studied — so a thing nobody has characterised has no risk group at all, rather than a high one.

Biosafety levels: a property of the work

  • BSL-1
    Open bench, good technique, handwashing, a door
  • BSL-2
    Biosafety cabinet for anything that aerosolises, restricted access, sharps rules, training, a sign on the door this is what most of this building runs at
  • BSL-3
    Directional airflow, sealed surfaces, respiratory protection, medical surveillance
  • BSL-4
    Suit, airlock, dedicated building. There are a handful in the country

You choose a level for an experiment. You do not assign one to an organism.

Select agents, and the toxin rule

  • A short federal list of agents judged a severe threat to public, animal or plant health — mostly potential bioterrorism agents. Bacillus anthracis, botulinum neurotoxin, the reconstructed 1918 influenza
  • It names organisms, proteins and small molecules — a toxin with no organism attached is still on the list
  • Toxins are regulated by amount: below the permissible quantity you are outside the rule, above it you are inside, with the same molecule
  • Registration, inventory, background checks, inspection. Violations carry fines, loss of permits, and for select agents, prison

If a select agent turns up in your design, that is a reason to redesign, not a reason to start paperwork.

Toxins and virulence factors

  • A toxin is regulated on potency and quantity. The form asks for an LD50 because that is the number the rule is written against
  • A virulence factor is a feature implicated in an organism’s ability to cause disease — adhesins, secretion systems, iron acquisition, immune evasion. There are databases of them
  • Neither is automatic: a sequence taken from a pathogen is not thereby regulated, and a sequence from a harmless organism is not thereby safe

When it goes wrong

  • 24 hours to report a serious incident to NIH — a laboratory-acquired infection, a loss of containment with risk to the community, an injury in BSL-3 or BSL-4, a significant violation
  • Incident reports and IBC minutes stay publicly posted for five years
  • A minor spill of a low-risk agent, cleaned and decontaminated with no breach of containment, generally does not need reporting
  • And the one nobody teaches: what you do. Stop, contain, wash or irrigate, tell your supervisor the same day, and get it written down — the record is what protects you later
