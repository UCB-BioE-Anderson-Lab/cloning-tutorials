slug: where_diversity_enters_an_eipcr_library
difficulty: easy
topic: Locating where diversity enters a construction
question: |
  In an EIPCR library, a pair of primers amplifies the whole plasmid outward and one primer carries degenerate bases in its 5' tail. Where does the diversity come from?
choices:
  A: Polymerase errors during the long amplification
  B: The template plasmid, which holds variants already
  C: The mixture of molecules in the degenerate primer
  D: The ligation, which joins ends in several registers
  E: The competent cells, which recombine the product
answer: C
explanation: |
  A degenerate oligo is not one sequence but a mixture synthesized in parallel, and every molecule in that mixture primes its own product. The library is therefore made at the moment of synthesis and merely carried through the PCR. Polymerase error is a different experiment with a far lower and uncontrolled rate, a template holding the variants already would mean the library existed before you built it, and the last two name sources of variation the workflow does not have.

slug: klenow_fill_in_for_a_short_cassette
difficulty: easy
topic: Recognizing Klenow fill-in as a route to a cassette
question: |
  Two partially complementary oligos are annealed and Klenow is added. What is Klenow doing?
choices:
  A: Amplifying the annealed pair exponentially
  B: Extending each 3' end on the other strand as template
  C: Cutting the duplex at the degenerate positions
  D: Ligating the two oligos into a closed circle
  E: Removing the random bases to leave a fixed sequence
answer: B
explanation: |
  The oligos overlap only in part, so annealing leaves recessed 3' ends. Klenow extends from each of those ends using the other strand as template, turning a partial duplex into a full one in a single isothermal step. There is no thermal cycling and so no exponential amplification, and the remaining options assign Klenow activities belonging to restriction enzymes, to ligase, and to nothing at all.

slug: degenerate_oligo_versus_oligo_pool
difficulty: medium
topic: Choosing between degenerate synthesis and a defined pool
question: |
  You want every single amino acid substitution at each of 250 positions, with no stops and no wild-type carryover. Which route fits?
choices:
  A: A chip-synthesized pool specifying each variant
  B: A degenerate oligo with NNN at every codon
  C: Error-prone PCR across the gene at a tuned rate
  D: A degenerate oligo with NNK at every codon
  E: DNA shuffling against the closest orthologs
answer: A
explanation: |
  The specification is a defined list, so the synthesis has to be defined too: an oligo pool writes each variant as its own sequence, which is what excludes stops and the wild type and gives one substitution per molecule. Both degenerate codes give random combinations across positions, including multiple substitutions per clone and stops, with no control over which members appear. Error-prone PCR controls neither position nor identity, and shuffling recombines variation rather than enumerating substitutions.

slug: reading_lowercase_tails_in_a_construction_oligo
difficulty: medium
topic: Reading a construction oligo and its tails
question: |
  A file digests a product with NheI and SalI. The forward oligo reads CAACTgctagcAAAGHDWCCTNRAAGGcatgaccgaaaccgtttctcg. What is the lowercase gctagc?
choices:
  A: The NheI site, added as a tail for cutting
  B: The ribosome binding site for the downstream gene
  C: The region that anneals to the template
  D: A spacer that only sets the product length
  E: The degenerate region that makes the library
answer: A
explanation: |
  By convention in these files the lowercase runs mark sequence added by the oligo rather than copied from the template, and GCTAGC is the NheI recognition sequence. The CAACT in front of it is the short flank the enzyme needs in order to cut near the end of a fragment. The degenerate region is the GHDW and NR stretch further along, the annealing region is the 3' end that matches the template, and nothing here is a spacer without function.

slug: soeing_places_diversity_at_a_junction
difficulty: medium
topic: Using overlap extension to place diversity at a junction
question: |
  You want a randomized linker between two protein domains that sit on separate plasmids. Why is SOEing a natural choice?
choices:
  A: The overlapping primers that join them carry the linker
  B: It removes the need to transform the product into cells
  C: It raises diversity beyond what the code allows
  D: It avoids restriction sites appearing in the linker
  E: It removes the need to sequence the finished library
answer: A
explanation: |
  In overlap extension the two fragments are joined through a sequence written into the primers, so whatever you put in that shared region ends up at the junction, and a degenerate linker is exactly that kind of insertion. Transformation and sequencing are needed at any scale, diversity is set by the degenerate code rather than by the assembly method, and while SOEing does avoid needing sites, the linker's content is a design choice either way.

slug: pp6_promoter_library_design
difficulty: medium
topic: Reading a promoter library design
question: |
  A promoter library randomizes the -35 and -10 boxes and holds the rest of the promoter fixed. Why those two regions?
choices:
  A: Sigma factor contacts them, so they set strength
  B: Synthesis cannot place degenerate bases elsewhere
  C: The sequence between them is not transcribed
  D: Randomizing the whole promoter would exceed one oligo
  E: The spacer between them cannot be varied at all
answer: A
explanation: |
  Sigma factor contacts the two hexamers, so changes there change how readily open complex forms, which is what a promoter strength library is sampling. Confining diversity to those positions also keeps the library small enough to cover while still spanning a wide range of strengths. The intervening spacer is part of the promoter and does affect strength, its length is a legitimate variable that this design simply does not use, and synthesis places degenerate bases wherever you ask.

slug: pwm_derived_library_purpose
difficulty: hard
topic: Using a position weight matrix to shape a design
question: |
  Instead of N at every position, you draw the degenerate code from a weight matrix built from known functional sites. What does that buy you?
choices:
  A: A guarantee that every member is functional
  B: A higher theoretical diversity for the same length
  C: A library concentrated on sequences likely to work
  D: Freedom from having to measure the resulting levels
  E: A library uniform in strength rather than spread
answer: C
explanation: |
  A weight matrix records which bases appear at which positions among sequences known to function, so a code drawn from it biases the library toward that region of sequence space, and the payoff is that a greater fraction of the clones you pick are worth measuring. A matrix is a statistical summary rather than a guarantee, constraining positions lowers diversity rather than raising it, the point is still to measure, and a spread of strengths is the goal.

slug: diversity_must_survive_the_cloning
difficulty: hard
topic: Checking that designed diversity survives construction
question: |
  Your degenerate bases sit in the primer tail upstream of the restriction site used for cloning. What happens to the library?
choices:
  A: Nothing, since primer tails are retained in the product
  B: The diversity moves into the backbone, not the insert
  C: The diversity is retained but inverted in the design
  D: The diversity doubles, as both orientations clone
  E: The digest discards it along with the outer flank
answer: E
explanation: |
  A restriction digest throws away everything outside the recognition site, including the short flank that was there to let the enzyme cut, so degenerate bases placed outside the site end up in the discarded fragment and every clone is identical. This is a real design error and the reason the degenerate region in a working oligo sits between the site and the annealing region. The other options describe rearrangements that do not occur.

slug: pooled_versus_arrayed_variant_construction
difficulty: hard
topic: Deciding between pooled and arrayed construction
question: |
  You need twelve specific promoter variants, each measured separately and kept as a named strain. Why build them one at a time?
choices:
  A: Degenerate synthesis cannot make only twelve sequences
  B: A pool would need deep sequencing to find and separate them
  C: Pooled libraries cannot be transformed into competent cells
  D: Twelve variants exceed what a single oligo can carry
  E: Named strains cannot be derived from a library at all
answer: B
explanation: |
  Pooling is worth the trouble when the number of variants is larger than you can handle individually. At twelve, building each on purpose gives identified strains immediately, while pooling means screening or sequencing to work out which clone is which and then isolating them. A small pool of defined oligos could in fact be ordered, pooled libraries are routinely transformed, named strains are routinely picked out of libraries, and twelve is far below any oligo limit.

slug: synthesis_route_for_a_whole_gene_variant_set
difficulty: hard
topic: Matching the synthesis route to the variation wanted
question: |
  You want to test forty orthologs of one enzyme, each a different natural sequence of about 1.2 kb. Which route fits?
choices:
  A: A degenerate oligo covering the differing positions
  B: Error-prone PCR of the host copy at a high rate
  C: EIPCR with a degenerate primer at the active site
  D: Chip synthesis of the forty genes, then assembly
  E: A Klenow fill-in of forty annealed oligo pairs
answer: D
explanation: |
  Forty full-length genes are forty defined sequences, which is the case chip synthesis exists to serve: the genes are written on a microarray, released as a pool, and selectively amplified and assembled. Orthologs differ at hundreds of positions in combinations no degenerate code can specify, mutagenesis of one sequence does not reproduce forty real ones, and a fill-in is limited to short cassettes far shorter than 1.2 kb.
