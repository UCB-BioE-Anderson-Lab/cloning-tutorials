slug: reading_a_weak_prototype
difficulty: easy
topic: Judging what makes a first-round result useful
question: |
  A first circuit gives output well below target, but it tracks inducer concentration in the direction and roughly the shape predicted. How should that be read?
choices:
  A: As a failure, since the output misses the target by a wide margin
  B: As inconclusive until the output reaches the specified level
  C: As a reason to abandon this architecture for a different one
  D: As a successful prototype, since the system behaves understandably
  E: As evidence the assay rather than the circuit needs attention
answer: D
explanation: |
  A prototype earns its keep by being interpretable, and a circuit responding in the predicted direction with roughly the predicted shape tells you the architecture works and only the magnitude needs attention, which is what later focused edits are for. A weak but well-behaved first result is a far better starting point than a strong one you cannot account for.

slug: prototype_versus_optimize
difficulty: easy
topic: Distinguishing the two modes
question: |
  What distinguishes optimization from prototyping in the design-build-test-learn cycle?
choices:
  A: Optimization starts from the current best and edits it narrowly
  B: Optimization uses a larger library than prototyping does
  C: Optimization is done in cells and prototyping in extract
  D: Optimization requires automation and prototyping does not
  E: Optimization happens before the first design is built
answer: A
explanation: |
  Prototyping builds a plausible design from scratch to see what happens, while optimization takes the construct that currently works best and makes focused changes to it, measuring each one against the last. The distinction is in where the design comes from and how far it moves, not in the format, the scale or the equipment used.

slug: why_part_boundaries_matter
difficulty: medium
topic: Explaining why part boundaries are designed in advance
question: |
  Why is it worth defining clean part boundaries and editable junctions in a first construct?
choices:
  A: Because they raise the expression level of the assembled circuit
  B: Because they reduce the number of transformants needed
  C: Because boundaries are required for the construct to be simulated
  D: Because later optimization means swapping parts one at a time
  E: Because they prevent recombination between repeated sequences
answer: D
explanation: |
  Optimization proceeds by changing one element and measuring, which is cheap if the promoter or the ribosome binding site can be exchanged on its own and expensive if the construct has to be rebuilt each time. Designing those seams in before you know which part you will want to change is what makes the later rounds fast, and it costs nothing at the first build.

slug: what_the_test_phase_returns
difficulty: medium
topic: Knowing what each phase of the cycle contributes
question: |
  In the design-build-test-learn cycle, what does the learn phase contribute?
choices:
  A: The assembled construct, ready to be assayed in the host
  B: The measurements of how the construct actually performed
  C: The specification the finished system has to satisfy
  D: The verification that the construct matches its design
  E: A revised understanding that changes the next design
answer: E
explanation: |
  Measurement is the test phase; learning is what you do with it, which is to revise your picture of the system so that the next design differs for a reason. A cycle that measures and then builds the next version without that step is iterating rather than learning, and tends to wander. The construct and its verification belong to the build.

slug: prototype_in_the_wrong_mode
difficulty: medium
topic: Recognizing an early design judged by the wrong standard
question: |
  A reviewer rejects a first-round circuit because its output falls short of the production target. What is wrong with that judgment?
choices:
  A: Nothing, since a design that misses its target has failed
  B: The target cannot be assessed without fermentation data
  C: A first design is built to be informative, not to hit a target
  D: The output should be normalized before it is compared
  E: Production targets apply only to pathways, not to circuits
answer: C
explanation: |
  Holding a prototype to a production specification mistakes which phase the work is in. The question to ask of a first design is whether it behaves understandably and tells you what to change, and a circuit that is weak but responds as predicted is a good prototype. Normalization matters for comparing measurements but is not what makes the judgment wrong.

slug: when_to_stop_prototyping
difficulty: hard
topic: Knowing when to move from prototyping to optimization
question: |
  What indicates that a project should move from prototyping into optimization?
choices:
  A: The first construct has been sequence verified successfully
  B: Automation has been set up for the construction workflow
  C: The available library size has been fully screened through
  D: A design behaves predictably and the limiting step is identified
  E: The output has reached the production specification already
answer: D
explanation: |
  Optimization is focused editing, and it pays only once you know what to edit, which means the system behaves in a way you can anticipate and you can say which step is holding the output back. Before that, further focused edits are guesses. Reaching the specification would mean the work is finished rather than entering a new phase.

slug: vector_editing_for_later_rounds
difficulty: hard
topic: Reasoning about designing for later edits
question: |
  Why design a vector so that a promoter can be replaced without rebuilding the whole construct?
choices:
  A: Because promoters are the only part worth varying later
  B: Because the vector will otherwise recombine during growth
  C: Because each optimization round then costs one cloning step
  D: Because replacing a promoter requires unique flanking sites
  E: Because the construct cannot otherwise be sequence verified
answer: C
explanation: |
  The economics of optimization are set by what each iteration costs, and a construct whose promoter sits between usable unique sites can be retuned with a single digest and ligation rather than a fresh assembly. Promoters are not the only part worth varying, which is an argument for designing several such seams rather than for ignoring them.

slug: learning_from_a_failed_prototype
difficulty: hard
topic: Extracting information from a design that did not work
question: |
  A prototype produces no detectable output at all. What is the most useful next step?
choices:
  A: Rebuild the same design and assay it a second time
  B: Move directly to optimizing the strongest available promoter
  C: Abandon the architecture and design a different circuit
  D: Test the parts separately to find which step is failing
  E: Raise the plasmid copy number and reassay the construct
answer: D
explanation: |
  A null result does not say where the fault lies, so the informative move is to decompose it: confirm that the reporter can be seen at all, that the promoter fires, and that each part behaves as a part before asking the assembly to work. Rebuilding unchanged tests reproducibility rather than cause, and tuning or abandoning the design commits before you know what is broken.

slug: cell_free_in_the_prototyping_phase
difficulty: hard
topic: Placing cell-free work within the cycle
question: |
  Why does cell-free expression sit naturally in the prototyping phase rather than in optimization?
choices:
  A: Because extracts give higher output than living cells do
  B: Because it tests parts quickly, without the context they will face
  C: Because optimization requires linkage that extracts cannot provide
  D: Because extracts cannot express more than one gene at a time
  E: Because prototypes are always built from linear DNA templates
answer: B
explanation: |
  Prototyping asks whether the parts do roughly what you think, and an extract answers that in hours from a PCR product, stripped of growth and native regulation. Optimization is about performance in the context the system will actually run in, so it has to be done there. Extracts do not outperform cells on yield and are not limited to a single gene.

slug: iteration_cost_drives_design
difficulty: hard
topic: Reasoning about what sets the pace of the cycle
question: |
  What most directly determines how many design-build-test-learn rounds a project can run?
choices:
  A: The theoretical diversity of the libraries being constructed
  B: The sequencing platform chosen for verifying each construct
  C: The number of parts in the registry available for use
  D: The time and cost of completing one full turn of the cycle
  E: The transformation efficiency of the chosen host strain
answer: D
explanation: |
  Rounds are the currency of this kind of work, and how many you get is set by how long and how much one of them takes, which is why so much effort goes into shortening the build and test steps through cloning strategy, automation and cell-free triage. Library diversity, part availability and transformation efficiency bear on individual steps rather than on the pace of the loop.
