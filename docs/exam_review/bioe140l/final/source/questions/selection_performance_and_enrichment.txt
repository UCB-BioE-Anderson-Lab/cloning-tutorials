slug: definition_of_enrichment
difficulty: easy
topic: Defining enrichment
question: |
  Enrichment for a selection step is defined as which of the following?
choices:
  A: The number of hits recovered after the selection is run
  B: The fraction of the hits that survive the selection step
  C: How strongly the selection prefers a hit over a non-hit
  D: The hit frequency after the step divided by the frequency before
  E: The number of rounds before hits become detectable at all
answer: D
explanation: |
  Enrichment is a ratio of frequencies, before and after, which is why it can be large even when very few molecules come through. The fraction of hits surviving is recovery and the preference for hits over non-hits is specificity; both feed into enrichment without being it. A raw count says nothing about the starting population, and the number of rounds is an observation about the campaign rather than a definition.

slug: definition_of_specificity_and_recovery
difficulty: easy
topic: Distinguishing specificity from recovery
question: |
  A step retains 90 percent of the hits and lets through 1 percent of the non-hits. Which statement names these correctly?
choices:
  A: Recovery is 90 percent; the preference for hits is specificity
  B: Specificity is 90 percent and the enrichment is 1 percent
  C: Recovery is 1 percent and specificity is 90 percent
  D: Both figures describe enrichment, which is their sum
  E: Recovery is 90 percent; specificity needs the round count
answer: A
explanation: |
  Recovery asks what fraction of the hits survive, which is the 90 percent. Specificity asks how strongly the step prefers a hit to a non-hit, which is what the contrast between 90 percent and 1 percent expresses. Two of the options simply swap the two terms, enrichment is determined by both without being their sum, and specificity is a property of a single step and needs no round count.

slug: enrichment_calculation_from_plates
difficulty: medium
topic: Computing enrichment from colony counts
question: |
  Before a sort you count 93 blue colonies and 1 red, red marking a hit. After it you count 3 blue and 183 red. Which expression gives the enrichment?
choices:
  A: (183 / 186) / (1 / 94), the two red frequencies
  B: 183 / 3, the red to blue ratio after the sort
  C: 183 / 1, the red counts after over before
  D: (183 + 3) / (93 + 1), the totals after over before
  E: (183 / 186) - (1 / 94), the change in red frequency
answer: A
explanation: |
  Enrichment is the frequency of hits after divided by the frequency before, so each plate is first turned into a frequency by dividing reds by the total of that plate, and the two frequencies are then divided. Raw counts depend on how much was plated and so cannot be compared directly, the red to blue ratio is not a frequency, and subtracting frequencies gives a difference rather than a fold change.

slug: why_multiple_rounds
difficulty: medium
topic: Explaining why selections run for several rounds
question: |
  SELEX and phage panning typically take five or six rounds. Why is one not enough?
choices:
  A: Because modest per-round gains against background must compound
  B: Because the target degrades and is replaced each round
  C: Because each round samples only a fraction of the library
  D: Because amplification cuts diversity, which later rounds restore
  E: Because the library has to be resynthesized between rounds
answer: A
explanation: |
  Non-specific binding carries much of the library through every round, so a single pass raises the hit frequency by a factor far short of what is needed to go from one in ten to the fifteenth to a workable pool, and repeating multiplies the enrichment. The pool is amplified rather than rebuilt, the whole library is passed over the target each round, and amplification does not restore diversity.

slug: compounding_enrichment_across_rounds
difficulty: medium
topic: Reasoning about enrichment over several rounds
question: |
  A step enriches about twentyfold. Starting from a hit frequency of one in a million, which expression gives the frequency after three rounds?
choices:
  A: 20^3 / 10^6, since the per-round factors multiply
  B: (20 x 3) / 10^6, since the per-round factors add
  C: 20 / 10^6, since the enrichment is a fixed property
  D: 10^6 / 20^3, since enrichment divides the frequency
  E: 20^3 x 10^6, since both quantities scale together
answer: A
explanation: |
  Each round multiplies the hit frequency by the enrichment, so three rounds contribute twenty cubed and the starting frequency is multiplied by that. Adding the per-round factors is the usual error and badly understates how fast a selection converges, using a single round ignores compounding altogether, and the remaining options divide or multiply in the wrong direction.

slug: recovery_versus_enrichment_tradeoff
difficulty: medium
topic: Trading recovery against enrichment
question: |
  You make the washes in a panning experiment much more stringent. What is the likely effect?
choices:
  A: Both the recovery and the enrichment per round fall
  B: Recovery rises, since only true binders remain bound
  C: Neither changes, as washing affects only the volume
  D: Enrichment falls, since fewer molecules are recovered
  E: Enrichment per round rises while recovery of real hits falls
answer: E
explanation: |
  Harsher washing discriminates more sharply, so the surviving pool is purer, but weakly binding real hits are lost along with the background. That trade is why stringency is tuned rather than maximized, and why early rounds are often run gently. Two of the options confuse the number of molecules recovered with the frequency of hits among them, and recovery refers to the hits specifically rather than to whatever remains bound.

slug: abundance_is_not_enrichment
difficulty: hard
topic: Separating abundance from enrichment in a sequenced pool
question: |
  Deep sequencing the final round shows ten sequences with by far the highest copy numbers. Why are these not necessarily your best binders?
choices:
  A: Because copy number cannot be measured accurately by reads
  B: Because the best binders are always rare in a final pool
  C: Because a sequence can be abundant for reasons other than selection
  D: Because the final round is always dominated by the input library
  E: Because abundance reflects affinity, which is not specificity
answer: C
explanation: |
  Abundance at the end is a snapshot with no before to compare against, and a sequence can be common because it synthesizes or amplifies well. In the published case the ten most abundant sequences in the final pool were the same ten in every round and showed no enrichment at all, while the real hits appeared infrequently early and climbed by thousands of fold. Rarity is not a rule, and the last option misdiagnoses the issue as affinity versus specificity.

slug: enrichment_requires_a_before_measurement
difficulty: hard
topic: Recognizing what an enrichment calculation requires
question: |
  You sorted a library into a pool that is 95 percent hits, but never plated the library beforehand. What can you report?
choices:
  A: The enrichment, as a naive library's frequency is negligible
  B: Both the specificity and the recovery of the sorting step
  C: The recovery, since 95 percent of what came through were hits
  D: Nothing quantitative can be reported about this experiment
  E: The final hit frequency, but not the enrichment
answer: E
explanation: |
  Enrichment is a ratio and the denominator is a measurement you either made or did not, so without the before plate you have a perfectly good final frequency and no enrichment. Substituting an assumption for that measurement is the habit worth breaking. Specificity and recovery would require tracking hits and non-hits separately, and the 95 percent is a purity rather than a fraction of hits retained.

slug: diagnosing_a_failed_selection
difficulty: hard
topic: Diagnosing a selection that fails to enrich
question: |
  After six rounds the eluted fraction has not risen and the pool is as diverse as the input. Which explanation fits?
choices:
  A: Recovery is too low, so hits were lost despite high specificity
  B: Enrichment was too high, collapsing the pool prematurely
  C: Amplification introduced too much diversity between rounds
  D: The target was immobilized at too high a density
  E: Either there are no hits, or the step has almost no specificity
answer: E
explanation: |
  Undiminished diversity and a flat elution signal mean the population is not being sorted at all, which happens when there is nothing to find or when the step cannot tell hits from background. Low recovery would reduce the amount recovered while still leaving the survivors skewed toward hits, excessive enrichment describes the opposite outcome, PCR cannot generate diversity on this scale, and dense target would raise binding rather than abolish it.

slug: negative_selection_in_a_dual_scheme
difficulty: hard
topic: Explaining why a positive selection alone is insufficient
question: |
  A positive selection with the new ligand clears everything that fails to activate the promoter. Why is a negative selection still needed?
choices:
  A: Because the positive round also removes the desired variants
  B: Because too few survivors remain after it to be sequenced
  C: Because the negative round raises recovery of the wanted clones
  D: Because wild type survives by answering the ligand it always could
  E: Because resistance to the drug decays over successive rounds
answer: D
explanation: |
  Survivors of the positive round are a mixture of genuinely reprogrammed variants and wild type, because activating the promoter is not by itself evidence of new specificity. The negative round, run with the old ligand and a counter-selectable marker, removes anything still answering the old stimulus. The negative round removes rather than retains clones, so it cannot raise recovery, and the remaining options contradict the purpose of the positive round or invent a mechanism.
