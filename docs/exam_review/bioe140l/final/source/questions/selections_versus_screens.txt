slug: definition_of_selection_versus_screen
difficulty: easy
topic: Distinguishing a selection from a screen
question: |
  What is the defining difference between a selection and a screen?
choices:
  A: A selection measures every member; a screen samples some
  B: A selection runs in vitro; a screen runs in living cells
  C: A selection reports a value per member; a screen reports survival
  D: In a selection hits persist and the rest are lost; a screen assays each
  E: A selection needs sequencing afterward and a screen does not
answer: D
explanation: |
  A selection applies a condition under which hits persist and non-hits are removed, so the library sorts itself and you never handle the losers. A screen assays members one at a time, so you learn something about each one but your effort scales with the library. Both run in vitro and in vivo, as SELEX and complementation show, and it is the screen rather than the selection that returns a quantitative value per member.

slug: why_large_libraries_need_selection
difficulty: easy
topic: Matching the method to library size
question: |
  Why are combinatorial libraries usually handled by selection rather than clone-by-clone screening?
choices:
  A: Because screening can never be made quantitative
  B: Because selections avoid needing genotype-phenotype linkage
  C: Because selections are cheaper but otherwise equivalent
  D: Because screening requires sequencing every member first
  E: Because they hold far more members than anyone can assay singly
answer: E
explanation: |
  A SELEX library runs to about ten to the fifteenth and a phage display library can reach ten to the eleventh, while picking and assaying clones by hand tops out in the hundreds. That mismatch in scale is the entire reason selections exist. A selection also discards the information a screen would have collected, so the two are not equivalent, and linkage is precisely what a pooled selection requires and a screen does not.

slug: rbs_library_can_be_screened
difficulty: medium
topic: Recognizing a library small enough to screen
question: |
  Some libraries, such as ribosome binding site libraries, are not too big to screen. What makes that true?
choices:
  A: Ribosome binding sites carry no real sequence diversity
  B: Such libraries cannot be selected, leaving screening only
  C: Expression can never be coupled to the survival of a cell
  D: Screening is practical only for libraries built by EIPCR
  E: A short degenerate region keeps the diversity in the hundreds
answer: E
explanation: |
  An RBS library with a handful of degenerate positions has a diversity in the hundreds or low thousands, so a plate of picked colonies samples enough of it to be informative. That decision is made from the theoretical diversity during design, which is why you calculate it before you build. Expression is readily coupled to a selectable marker, so selection remains available, and the construction method has no bearing on the scale.

slug: diversity_calculation_precedes_the_choice
difficulty: medium
topic: Deciding screen or selection during design
question: |
  When should you decide whether to screen or select, and on what basis?
choices:
  A: After transformation, from how many colonies appeared
  B: During design, from the theoretical diversity of the library
  C: After the first assays, from whether any hits were found
  D: During design, from which sequencing platform is available
  E: It does not matter, as a screen converts to a selection later
answer: B
explanation: |
  The theoretical diversity is known from the design before any DNA is made, and it determines whether a clone-by-clone assay can cover the library at all. Deciding afterward means you may have built something you have no way to interrogate. The colony count conflates coverage with method and comes too late, the platform is a downstream consideration, and converting a screen to a selection requires a genotype-phenotype link most screens do not provide.

slug: enrichment_then_individual_characterization
difficulty: medium
topic: Sequencing selection and characterization
question: |
  After the rounds of a SELEX are complete and the eluted fraction has climbed, what happens next?
choices:
  A: The pool is reported, as selection already found the best binder
  B: Further rounds are run until a single sequence remains
  C: The pool is cloned, members are picked, sequenced and characterized
  D: The pool is screened clone by clone before selection is applied
  E: The pool is discarded and a fresh library is selected again
answer: C
explanation: |
  A selection narrows a library from an unmanageable size to a pool small enough to handle individually, and that is where clone-by-clone work begins: clone the pool, pick members, sequence them, and measure affinity on their own. Reporting the pool treats enrichment as a final answer when it still contains non-specific binders, and running to a single sequence is neither how the rounds behave nor the goal.

slug: selection_discards_information
difficulty: medium
topic: Recognizing what a selection does not tell you
question: |
  A selection recovers hits from a library of ten million. What does it not tell you that a screen would have?
choices:
  A: The sequences of the clones that were recovered
  B: Whether the recovered clones are viable at all
  C: The theoretical diversity of the starting library
  D: A value for every member, including failures and by how much
  E: Nothing, as a selection yields strictly more than a screen
answer: D
explanation: |
  Non-hits are physically removed in a selection, so no record survives of how any of them performed; you learn which members passed a threshold and nothing about the distribution behind it. That is the trade you accept in exchange for handling a library no screen could cover. Recovered hits are of course sequenced, and viability and theoretical diversity are unaffected by the choice of method.

slug: screen_scale_arithmetic
difficulty: medium
topic: Comparing library size against screening capacity
question: |
  Your library has diversity near a million and one clone takes a minute to pick and assay. Why is a screen not an option?
choices:
  A: Because the assay loses sensitivity at that scale
  B: Because libraries above ten thousand cannot be transformed
  C: Because a million minutes is on the order of two years
  D: Because the clones would be too similar to tell apart
  E: Because screens need a selectable marker this library lacks
answer: C
explanation: |
  The argument is arithmetic rather than biology: a million one-minute assays is roughly two years of continuous work, which settles the matter before any question of sensitivity arises. Recognizing when the scale alone rules out a method is the point. Libraries far above ten thousand are transformed routinely, and it is selections rather than screens that need the marker.

slug: fluorescence_sorting_as_a_selection
difficulty: hard
topic: Classifying a sort as a selection
question: |
  You scrape a library off a plate, sort by fluorescence, and keep the brightest fraction. Is that a screen or a selection?
choices:
  A: A screen, because fluorescence is measured on each cell
  B: A selection, as the pool is partitioned with no record kept
  C: Neither, since cytometry analyzes rather than enriches
  D: A screen, because none of the cells are actually killed
  E: A selection only if a kill step such as a drug is included
answer: B
explanation: |
  The instrument does measure each cell, which is what makes the screen answer tempting, but no per-clone value is retained and the unselected cells are discarded: what comes out is an enriched population rather than a table of results. Survival is not what defines a selection; partitioning without a per-member record is. Sorting is used to enrich all the time, so it is not merely analytical.

slug: arraying_as_the_middle_ground
difficulty: hard
topic: Placing arrayed screening between picking and selection
question: |
  You need per-variant expression values for about two thousand library members. Which workflow fits?
choices:
  A: A pooled selection, as two thousand is too many to handle
  B: Picking and assaying the clones one at a time in tubes
  C: SELEX against the target protein immobilized on a column
  D: Sorting the pool and keeping only the brightest fraction
  E: A colony picker arraying into plates read on a plate reader
answer: E
explanation: |
  The requirement is a value for each member, which rules out anything pooled, and two thousand is well within reach of a colony picker and a few dozen plates. This is the regime automation exists to serve. Pooling and sorting both discard the per-variant values that were asked for, picking by hand would work but means two thousand manual assays, and a binding selection reports nothing about expression.

slug: selection_cannot_report_a_distribution
difficulty: hard
topic: Matching the method to the question rather than the scale
question: |
  You want the distribution of promoter strengths across fifty thousand variants, not just the strongest. Why is a growth selection a poor fit?
choices:
  A: Fifty thousand members is too small a library to select on
  B: Resistance markers cannot be driven by a variable promoter
  C: Selections require long reads, unavailable at this scale
  D: It reports only whether each variant cleared a threshold
  E: Promoter strength cannot be linked to survival in any way
answer: D
explanation: |
  The question asked for a distribution, and a survival selection gives a binary outcome per variant set by the drug concentration. The right instrument is a pooled measurement returning counts per variant, such as barcode sequencing of transcripts, which preserves the whole range. Coupling promoter strength to a resistance marker is exactly how such selections are built, so the coupling objections are false.
