slug: diversity_is_a_product
difficulty: easy
topic: Computing theoretical diversity from degenerate positions
question: |
  A cassette carries the degenerate stretch S-W-H, where S is two bases, W is two and H is three. Which expression gives its theoretical diversity?
choices:
  A: 2 x 2 x 3, multiplying the options at each position
  B: 2 + 2 + 3, summing the options at each position
  C: 3^3, three options at three positions
  D: 3^2, three positions squared
  E: (2 x 2) + 3, grouping the pair first
answer: A
explanation: |
  Each position varies independently within one molecule, so the number of distinct sequences is the product across positions. Adding gives the number of options you have altogether, not the number of combinations, and it is the most common error on this kind of question. Treating every position as three-fold ignores the specific codes, and squaring the position count inverts the roles of bases and positions.

slug: choosing_a_code_for_a_target_diversity
difficulty: easy
topic: Working backward from a target diversity
question: |
  You want exactly 16 variants from two degenerate positions. Which pair of codes gives that?
choices:
  A: N and N, since 4 x 4 is 16
  B: N and S, since 4 x 2 is 16
  C: W and W, since 2 x 2 is 16
  D: N and D, since 4 x 3 is 16
  E: H and H, since 3 x 3 is 16
answer: A
explanation: |
  N stands for all four bases, so two N positions give four times four. The others state their own arithmetic correctly but reach 8, 4, 12 and 9 rather than 16, so the work is in knowing how many bases each letter covers: N is four, D and H and V and B are three, and the rest are two.

slug: nnk_versus_nnn_codon_choice
difficulty: easy
topic: Why NNK is used for saturation mutagenesis
question: |
  Why does a saturation oligo usually specify NNK at each codon rather than NNN?
choices:
  A: NNK removes every stop codon from the library
  B: NNK still covers all twenty amino acids but leaves only one stop
  C: NNK gives all twenty amino acids an equal chance of appearing
  D: NNK is the only code a DNA synthesizer makes reliably
  E: NNK restricts the library to the wild-type amino acids
answer: B
explanation: |
  NNK is 32 codons rather than 64. It still encodes all twenty amino acids, but it carries one stop instead of three, so a smaller library carries a smaller dead fraction. The attractive wrong answer is that it removes every stop: the amber stop survives in NNK. Codon redundancy still differs between amino acids, so leucine and serine stay over-represented relative to methionine and tryptophan.

slug: transformation_is_the_ceiling
difficulty: medium
topic: Identifying the step that limits library size
question: |
  A degenerate library is amplified by PCR, ligated and transformed. Which step sets the practical ceiling on how many distinct variants you can screen?
choices:
  A: The number of cycles in the amplification
  B: The plasmid copy number maintained within each cell
  C: The sequencing depth used to check the library
  D: The synthesis scale of the degenerate oligo
  E: The transformation, since colonies bound distinct clones
answer: E
explanation: |
  The DNA coming off a synthesizer and through PCR contains far more distinct molecules than any design calls for. What you can clone, grow and assay is bounded by how many independent transformants you obtain, which is why library construction is reported in transformants. Cycles amplify copies rather than variety, copy number is copies per cell rather than variants per library, and ordinary synthesis yields picomoles of molecules.

slug: coverage_factor_meaning
difficulty: medium
topic: Interpreting the coverage requirement
question: |
  For a library of diversity n you are told to collect about 4.6 x n transformants. What does that factor mean?
choices:
  A: The point at which about 99 percent of members appear at least once
  B: The fold-excess of oligo over template in the PCR
  C: The number of colonies you must sequence to confirm the design
  D: The point at which every member is present exactly once
  E: The number of pooled reactions the construction requires
answer: A
explanation: |
  Sampling n variants at random follows a Poisson distribution, and the chance a given member is missed after sampling k x n times is about e to the minus k. Setting that to one percent gives k of about 4.6. Exactly one copy each is impossible, since random sampling piles copies onto some members while missing others, and the remaining options attach the factor to steps it has nothing to do with.

slug: coverage_at_two_fold_sampling
difficulty: medium
topic: Estimating the missing fraction from sampling depth
question: |
  Your library has diversity n and you recover 2 x n transformants. Roughly what fraction of designed members is absent?
choices:
  A: None, since you collected twice as many colonies as members
  B: About 1 percent, the standard coverage target
  C: About half, since colonies are shared among duplicates
  D: About e to the minus 2, or roughly an eighth
  E: About seven eighths, the complement of the covered share
answer: D
explanation: |
  At a sampling depth of k times the diversity the expected fraction missed is about e to the minus k, and e to the minus 2 is a little over 0.13. Expecting none is the intuition the Poisson result exists to correct, since random sampling misses some members entirely. One percent is the right target quoted at the wrong depth, which needs about 4.6 x n, and seven eighths reports the covered share as the missing one.

slug: scaling_degeneracy_and_reaction_count
difficulty: medium
topic: Separating diversity from the number of reactions
question: |
  You widen a degenerate oligo so the diversity rises about a thousandfold. Which downstream quantity scales with that change?
choices:
  A: The transformants needed to cover the library
  B: The number of degenerate oligos you must order
  C: The number of pooled construction reactions
  D: The number of ligations the workflow requires
  E: The volume of competent cells per transformation
answer: A
explanation: |
  A degenerate oligo is a mixture of molecules, so one reaction containing that mixture produces the whole library however wide it is. Diversity changes what is in the tube, not how many tubes there are, and the same is true of the oligo order and the ligation. What does scale is coverage: more transformants to sample the larger design, and more clones or reads to characterize it.

slug: theoretical_versus_held_diversity
difficulty: medium
topic: Distinguishing designed diversity from the library in hand
question: |
  You design a library of diversity one million, transform it, and count ten thousand colonies. How large is the library you can actually screen?
choices:
  A: One million, since the ligation contains them all
  B: Ten billion, the design times the colony count
  C: One hundred, the square root of the colony count
  D: At most ten thousand, since each colony is one clone
  E: Indeterminate without sequencing the ligation
answer: D
explanation: |
  The ligation probably does contain nearly all the designed sequences, and that fact does not help: what you can screen is the set of clones you isolated. Ten thousand colonies is at most ten thousand distinct variants, and fewer in practice because of resampling. Reporting the design rather than the experiment is the error here, and the colony count already gives a hard upper bound without any sequencing.

slug: synthesis_bias_versus_designed_diversity
difficulty: hard
topic: Reasoning about bias between design and realization
question: |
  Sequencing shows variants carrying G at one degenerate position are threefold commoner than those carrying T. What does that do to the transformants you need?
choices:
  A: Nothing, since coverage depends only on diversity
  B: Lowers it, since the library is less diverse than designed
  C: Makes the library unusable, as biased pools cannot be screened
  D: Raises it, since the rarest members need deeper sampling
  E: Raises it only if the bias falls in the coding sequence
answer: D
explanation: |
  The 4.6 x n rule assumes uniform sampling. Once some members are systematically rarer, those are the ones that go missing, and you must sample deeper to be confident they are present. The bias comes from the phosphoramidite mixture at the degenerate position and from amplification, and it is why a library is worth sequencing before it is trusted. Skew is not reduced diversity, since the member count is unchanged, and biased libraries are screened all the time.

slug: coverage_target_versus_screening_capacity
difficulty: hard
topic: Reconciling coverage with screening capacity
question: |
  Your design has diversity five thousand and you can assay four hundred clones. Which conclusion follows?
choices:
  A: The screen is adequate, since the clones sample randomly
  B: Shrink the design, or replace the screen with a selection
  C: Pick the four hundred and report the best as the optimum
  D: Raise the transformants to 4.6 x n, which fixes the problem
  E: Sequence the four hundred to recover the missing members
answer: B
explanation: |
  Four hundred clones out of five thousand designed members samples under a tenth of the library, so the best variant is probably never tested. The two honest fixes are to shrink the design until the screen can cover it, or to hand the whole population to a selection or a sort. Random sampling is not adequate sampling, a sampled maximum is not the true one, raising transformants fixes coverage of the freezer rather than of the assay, and nothing recovers variants that were never assayed.
