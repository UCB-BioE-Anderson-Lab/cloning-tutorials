slug: rbs_library_expected_outcome
difficulty: easy
topic: Predicting the phenotype distribution a library produces
question: |
  You place a library of ribosome binding sites upstream of a reporter gene in E. coli and plate the transformants. What is the most likely outcome?
choices:
  A: Every clone expresses the reporter at the same level but reaches it at a different time.
  B: The clones span a range of reporter levels across the population.
  C: Only clones with a strong ribosome binding site express at all, because the promoter represses the rest.
  D: The clones differ in plasmid copy number rather than in reporter level.
  E: The reporter level is unchanged, because the ribosome binding site acts on transcription.
answer: B
explanation: |
  Changing the ribosome binding site changes how efficiently ribosomes initiate on the message, so the library produces a spread of protein levels. That spread is the product, which is why these libraries are built. A substitutes timing for level with no mechanism. C invents repression that is not in the design. D confuses a translational element with the origin of replication. E misassigns the ribosome binding site to transcription.

slug: promoter_library_acts_on_transcription
difficulty: easy
topic: Matching a genetic element to the step it controls
question: |
  A promoter library and a ribosome binding site library both give a range of protein output. At which step does each act?
choices:
  A: Both act on transcription, differing only in magnitude.
  B: The promoter library acts on transcription; the ribosome binding site library acts on translation initiation.
  C: The promoter library acts on translation; the ribosome binding site library acts on mRNA stability.
  D: Both act on translation, since protein is the readout in each case.
  E: The promoter library acts on copy number; the ribosome binding site library acts on transcription.
answer: B
explanation: |
  The promoter determines how often RNA polymerase initiates, so a promoter library varies the amount of message. The ribosome binding site determines how readily the ribosome loads onto that message, so an RBS library varies protein made per message. Both end up changing protein, which is why D is tempting, but the step being varied is what the question asks. A, C and E each swap the two assignments or substitute copy number.

slug: tuning_a_ratio_within_an_operon
difficulty: medium
topic: Choosing which element to vary for a stated goal
question: |
  Two enzymes are expressed from one operon behind a single promoter, and you need more of the first and less of the second. Which change gives you that?
choices:
  A: Replace the promoter with a stronger one.
  B: Vary the ribosome binding site in front of the second gene.
  C: Move the operon to a higher copy number origin.
  D: Add a second copy of the promoter upstream of the operon.
  E: Change the codon usage of both genes together.
answer: B
explanation: |
  Everything upstream of the operon acts on both genes at once, so a stronger promoter or a higher copy number raises both and leaves the ratio where it was. The ribosome binding site sits in front of one coding sequence and sets translation of that one, which is the only element in this list that can move the two independently. A, C and D all scale the pair. E acts on both genes and is a blunt instrument for level in any case.

slug: terminator_library_readout
difficulty: medium
topic: Identifying the measured quantity for a terminator library
question: |
  You build a library of terminator sequences between an upstream and a downstream fluorescent reporter. What does the experiment measure for each library member?
choices:
  A: The absolute brightness of the upstream reporter.
  B: The fraction of transcripts that read through the terminator, read as the ratio of downstream to upstream signal.
  C: The plasmid copy number of each clone.
  D: The rate at which the upstream reporter protein folds.
  E: The translation initiation rate of the downstream reporter.
answer: B
explanation: |
  A terminator is judged by how much transcription it stops, and the way to see that without being confused by clone-to-clone differences in copy number or growth is to put a reporter on each side and take a ratio. The upstream reporter is the internal reference and the downstream one reports read-through. A would vary with copy number and growth rather than with the terminator. C, D and E name quantities the construct does not isolate.

slug: rbs_library_with_no_effect
difficulty: medium
topic: Interpreting a library that produces no spread
question: |
  You screen a ribosome binding site library in front of an enzyme and find that every clone gives the same pathway output. Which explanation is most plausible?
choices:
  A: The ribosome binding site does not affect translation in E. coli.
  B: The enzyme is not the step limiting pathway output, so changing its level changes nothing downstream.
  C: The library has no diversity, since identical output implies identical sequence.
  D: The reporter must be measured by cytometry rather than a plate reader.
  E: The promoter must have been deleted during cloning.
answer: B
explanation: |
  The library almost certainly produced a range of enzyme levels; what the experiment shows is that pathway output does not respond to that range, which is what it looks like when some other step is the bottleneck. A contradicts the premise of the method. C is the right thing to check but the wrong inference, since sequencing would settle it and identical output has an easier explanation. D changes the instrument, not the result, for a bulk pathway measurement. E would abolish output rather than fix it at one level.

slug: copy_number_versus_element_strength
difficulty: medium
topic: Separating dosage from element strength
question: |
  You move a reporter construct from a p15A origin to a high-copy origin and the signal rises fourfold. Your colleague concludes the promoter is stronger. What is wrong with that?
choices:
  A: Nothing; promoter strength and copy number are the same quantity.
  B: Copy number multiplies everything on the plasmid, so per-template initiation rate is unchanged.
  C: High-copy origins silence promoters, so the signal should have fallen.
  D: The comparison is invalid because fluorescence cannot be compared between strains.
  E: The promoter is weaker, since the same output is now spread over more templates.
answer: B
explanation: |
  Promoter strength is a property of one template: how often polymerase initiates there. Raising copy number gives you more templates, each initiating at the same rate, so total output rises while the promoter is untouched. This is the distinction every expression comparison depends on. A collapses two different quantities. C invents an effect. D is too strong, since the comparison is routine with proper normalization. E confuses total with per-template but draws the opposite wrong conclusion.

slug: library_is_a_distribution_not_an_answer
difficulty: hard
topic: Treating a library as a distribution to be sampled
question: |
  A colleague says the purpose of a ribosome binding site library is to find the strongest ribosome binding site. What is the better description of what the library gives you?
choices:
  A: A distribution of expression levels, from which the screen picks whichever level the application needs.
  B: A single optimal sequence, which sequencing of the pool reveals directly.
  C: A measurement of the maximum expression the host can sustain.
  D: A set of clones all at the same level, differing only in sequence.
  E: A prediction of expression level from sequence, usable without any measurement.
answer: A
explanation: |
  The library is a population spanning a range, and what counts as the best member depends on the goal: a pathway enzyme may need a middling level, and a toxic product may need a weak one. Treating the library as a search for a maximum discards most of its value. B is wrong because sequencing the pool tells you what is there, not what works. C is a different experiment. D contradicts the method. E describes a model, which libraries are used to build rather than to replace.

slug: toxic_product_distorts_a_recovered_library
difficulty: hard
topic: Recognizing survivor bias in a recovered library
question: |
  You build a ribosome binding site library in front of a gene whose product is toxic above a certain level, transform, and grow the transformants overnight before picking. What does the recovered library look like relative to the one you designed?
choices:
  A: Unchanged, since toxicity affects growth rate rather than survival.
  B: Depleted of the strongest members, because those clones grow poorly or not at all.
  C: Depleted of the weakest members, because they are outcompeted.
  D: Enriched for the strongest members, since high expression gives a selective advantage.
  E: Unchanged in composition but uniformly lower in copy number.
answer: B
explanation: |
  Overnight outgrowth is a selection whether or not you intended one. Clones expressing the toxic product most strongly divide slowly or die, so the high end of the designed distribution is thinned out or missing from what you pick. This is the same logic as plasmid loss under antibiotic: the population you measure is the population that survived the measurement. A understates it, since a growth-rate difference over many generations becomes a composition difference. C and D have the direction backwards. E invents an unrelated effect.

slug: why_an_rbs_library_rather_than_codon_changes
difficulty: medium
topic: Choosing a tuning handle with a predictable effect
question: |
  You need to tune the level of one enzyme across a wide range. Why is a ribosome binding site library a better handle than recoding the gene with alternative synonymous codons?
choices:
  A: Codon changes cannot alter protein level at all.
  B: The ribosome binding site is a small, separable element whose variation spans a wide range without touching the protein sequence.
  C: Codon changes require chip synthesis, which is unavailable for genes this size.
  D: The ribosome binding site determines the protein's folding, which codons do not.
  E: Synonymous codon changes alter the amino acid sequence and so confound the result.
answer: B
explanation: |
  Varying a short element upstream of the start codon gives a broad, roughly monotonic range of initiation rates while leaving the coding sequence untouched, which keeps the enzyme itself out of the comparison. Recoding does shift expression, but through several entangled mechanisms including message structure and elongation, and across the whole gene. A and E are false; synonymous changes do affect expression and by definition do not change the protein. C is false. D misattributes folding to the ribosome binding site.

slug: holding_the_context_constant_in_a_part_library
difficulty: hard
topic: Controlling the context around a varied part
question: |
  In the course's terminator work, each candidate terminator is cloned into the same position in the same reporter plasmid rather than tested in whatever vector it arrived in. Why does that matter?
choices:
  A: It raises the theoretical diversity of the library.
  B: It keeps everything other than the part under test identical, so differences in signal can be assigned to the part.
  C: It is required for the parts to be sequenced by cycle sequencing.
  D: It increases the copy number of the construct and so the signal.
  E: It eliminates the need for an upstream reference reporter.
answer: B
explanation: |
  A part's measured behavior depends on its surroundings: promoter, copy number, growth, and the reporters on either side. Putting every candidate into one fixed context makes the measurements comparable with each other, which is the whole premise of characterizing parts. A has nothing to do with it. C is false. D is incidental and not the reason. E is backwards, since the internal reference is still needed to handle clone-to-clone variation.
