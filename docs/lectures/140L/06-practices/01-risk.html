Risk, and what we know

Fifty years of nothing going wrong

Known and unknown risks

  • known knowns
    Hazards we can name and have measured an infectious dose, an LD50, a route of transmission — these are what the rules are written against
  • known unknowns
    Questions we know to ask and cannot yet answer what a characterised gene does in a host nobody has put it in
  • unknown unknowns
    Harm by a mechanism nobody has thought to ask about the one the field is actually afraid of

A taxonomy is not a measurement. The third row has no size — which is exactly why arguments about it do not resolve.

Phrasing after Donald Rumsfeld, then United States Secretary of Defense, answering a question in a press briefing.

Fifty years, and no major incident

The claim we make without making it

A laboratory E. coli overexpressing GFP cannot be dangerous. Everyone in this room believes this. It is probably true.

  • The mechanistic basis for ruling out harm in such an organism has never been established
  • What we are holding is a habit of classification, not an argument. It happens to give the right answer here
  • Noticing the difference is the skill. A field that designs from knowledge rather than from precedent has to be able to say why

Cambridge, 1976

  • The Cambridge City Council — the elected city government, not the universities — held public hearings on whether recombinant DNA work could be done inside the city limits
  • Three years after the first recombinant plasmids. There was no track record to argue from. Not fifty years of it. Not one
  • It ended in a temporary local moratorium and a citizens’ review board — a city, not a funding agency, writing the first rules

The mirror image of the last slide: there, a long record and no mechanism. Here, no record at all — and the argument had to be made anyway.
