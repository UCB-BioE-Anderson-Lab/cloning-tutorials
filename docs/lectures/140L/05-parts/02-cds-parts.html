What makes a protein a reporter

→A reporter is a protein you can quantify in a living cell without knowing anything about the cell.

“Reporter” is not a kind of protein. Any protein is an enzyme, a binding protein, a transporter, a scaffold — a reporter is one you happen to be able to read.

Two ways to be readable: the protein is the signal, or the protein makes the signal from something you add. The second one amplifies. The first one does not.

Fluorescent proteins — the protein is the signal

→GFP, BFP, YFP, RFP, and luciferase, which makes its own light rather than needing any shone at it.

One molecule of GFP gives one molecule’s worth of signal. There is no turnover, so the number you read is the number of molecules — which is exactly what makes it good for quantification and bad for detecting something rare.

This is BestP. Every number you record this week is a fluorescent protein saying a promoter fired — which is why you quote it against J23101 and not on its own.

LacZ — the protein makes the signal

Alkaline phosphatase — a reporter that is also a probe

→PhoA strips phosphates off almost anything, and folds into an active enzyme only in the periplasm.

Chassis §6 works through why: the disulphides it needs cannot form in the cytoplasm. Chassis §6.

So it reports where, not how much — and choosing a reporter is really choosing what its silence will mean.

Selectable markers — three mechanisms

Transcription factors

→A protein that binds a particular DNA sequence and changes what transcription does there.

global — responds to the cell’s state

  • CAP · carbon source
  • Fur · iron
  • H-NS, IHF · chromosome structure
  • two-component systems · what is outside

local — responds to one ligand

  • LacI · lactose
  • AraC · arabinose
  • NahR · salicylate
  • VirF · virulence genes

You build with the right-hand column. A global regulator drags the rest of the cell along with it — NahR was the right answer because it is local.
