Why biology is now a software problem, the Design–Build–Test–Learn cycle the course follows, and what an AI model does and does not do for you when the hard part is the biology.

2Schemas and Models

The move the whole course is built on, taught on a plastic egg before the biology gets hard: name the nouns, write the schema, say what the abstraction throws away. Classes and instances, state, and immutability as a design choice.

3CRISPR I — The Biology

Genomes, plasmids and oligos as sources of DNA; the Cas9–guide–target complex; what the PAM is and why it exists; what happens after the cut, and what a design tool can reasonably ignore.

4CRISPR II — Design Logic and Encoding a Rule

Turning the mechanism into a function, and surfacing every judgment call on the way: which protospacer, which strand, and when the right move is not to design a guide at all. Guard clauses, failing loudly, and the difference between an error and an empty result.

5Validation as Science

A test is an empirical claim. Deriving test cases from published, experimentally-validated constructs, why “it runs” is not “it is right,” and the cheap correctness properties worth asserting even when you cannot check the answer.

6Composition and Construct

Strain, genotype and Composition are three different things. Composition as a specification rather than an implementation — and an explicit account of what it leaves out: expression level, regulatory parts, codon usage, plasmid or genome.

Expression The design problem, one constraint at a time — and the predicates that catch each one.

7The Central Dogma as Engineering Constraint

Transcription and translation from the designer's side, where every element is a knob that can be set wrong. Promoter, ribosome binding site, coding sequence, terminator, and the regions that actually govern how much protein you get.

8Translation Initiation and the RBS

The most failure-prone step in heterologous expression. Shine–Dalgarno pairing with 16S rRNA, spacing and its tolerance, occlusion by secondary structure, and proteomics as objective evidence that a sequence expresses.

9Two Models of the RBS

A biophysical free-energy model and a curated set of evolved sequences, solving the same problem from opposite directions. What each one buys, what each one costs, and how you would find out which to trust.

10Checkers I — Biology as a Predicate

The load-bearing idea of the course. A checker is a biological claim made executable and falsifiable; centralising that logic is what keeps an algorithm and its tests from drifting apart. Choosing a threshold, and what makes a threshold defensible.

11What Actually Predicts Expression

The randomised-codon experiments: a 250-fold range from codon choice alone, full-length codon adaptation index that does not correlate with expression, and folding energy near the start that does. Which checkers this justifies, and how heavily.

12Checkers II — Motifs, PWMs, and Calibration

The harder predicates, where the threshold is a real scientific judgment. Internal ribosome binding sites, position weight matrices for σ70 promoters calibrated against a known promoter family, and false-positive rate against a real proteome as the correctness signal.

13Codon Usage, CAI, and RSCU

The arithmetic of codon bias made concrete: normalised codon frequency, the relative adaptiveness parameter, codon adaptation index as a geometric mean, what RSCU measures instead, and why adjacent codons matter too.

Fabrication Searching a design space, and where the constraints came from in the first place.

14Codon Optimization Algorithms

From scoring one sequence to searching a space of them. One-codon lookup, enumerate-and-rank and its combinatorial wall, guided random selection, and the sliding window — with the checkers as the fitness function.

15Fabrication and Assembly

Where the forbidden sequences come from. PCR and what a product is specified by, isothermal and Type IIS assembly, the length limits of commercial and DIY synthesis, and how a fabrication constraint propagates backwards into a design rule.

16MCP — Wrapping Capabilities for LLMs

Making a function callable by a language model. Client, server and the protocol between them; tools, resources and prompts; and what belongs in a semantic wrapper — purpose, constraints, non-goals, guarantees — as opposed to a developer spec.

17Project Scope

What a good project scope looks like and what a bad one looks like: what is in, what is out, what it depends on, and how anyone will know when it is right.

Build, Test, Learn Down the ladder: each layer supplies what the one above it discarded.

18Construction Files

The abstraction that separates molecular biology from lab implementation. Reference by identifier, the shorthand and its operations, and an explicit account of what a construction file deliberately omits — tubes, volumes, temperatures, buffers.

19LabPlanner, Inventory, and Robotics

The layer below, where everything the construction file discarded has to be supplied. LabSheets, inventory and sample location, mastermixes and tip reuse, liquid-handler APIs, and human burden and cost as objective functions.

20Prototyping and Optimization

Building something new and improving something that exists are different design problems. Single-part edit libraries, promoter and ortholog scans, and why a combinatorial space you cannot enumerate still has a tractable edge.

21Analysis — LCMS and Inference

Closing the loop. Chromatography and mass spectrometry far enough to read the data; targeted analysis against a defined set of chemicals of interest; feeding experiments as positive controls; and what a table of peak calls does and does not license you to conclude.

22Atoms, Molecules, and Reactions

The same analytical move, one level below biology. A molecule as a graph, a canonical identifier as a hash, a reaction as a difference between two states, reactive centres, and patterns and transformations over chemical structure.

23Synthesis, Reachability, and the Limits of Abstraction

Expanding a metabolic network shell by shell to find what a cell could make; tracing back from a target to the routes that reach it; and reaction operators, which keep the mechanism and throw away the active site — which is exactly why they predict far more chemistry than any enzyme will actually do.
