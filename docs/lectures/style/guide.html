140L Slide Style Guide

A proposed specification for the lecture series

This describes how the 140L decks should look and behave — not how any one of them currently does. It sets an accessibility conformance target, a visual language, and the rules that follow from both. Blue panels are requirements. Amber panels are open decisions worth settling before the series grows.

1  Audience and conditions

Design decisions only make sense against the conditions the slides actually meet. Assume all of these simultaneously:

ConditionConsequence
Projected in a lit lecture hall Low effective contrast. Mid-tones wash out; hairlines disappear.
Viewed from the back row Type below ~1/30 of slide height is unreadable. Detail below ~1/200 vanishes.
~8% of the men present have a red–green deficiency Hue alone cannot separate categories.
Recorded and rewatched at small size Must survive being a quarter of its authored size.
Sometimes printed, often in monochrome Everything must survive greyscale.
Read later without narration A slide must carry its own meaning; the notes are not always present.

Every rule below traces back to one of these. Where a rule looks fussy, it is because two of these conditions apply at once.

2  Principles

Five, in priority order. When rules conflict, the earlier principle wins.

  1. Accuracy before beauty. A diagram is an argument about a mechanism. If it is elegant and wrong, it is worse than a crude sketch that is right — students remember pictures longer than captions.
  2. One idea per slide. If a slide needs two sentences of narration to explain what is on it, it is two slides.
  3. Ink is expensive. Every mark must carry information. Decoration, gradients, shadows, borders-for-their-own-sake, and background texture are prohibited outright.
  4. Emphasis is relative. Nothing is emphasised unless something else is not. This is why colour is an attention ladder (§4) rather than a set of category labels.
  5. Motion must mean something. Animate a change; never animate an arrival.

The aesthetic that follows is deliberately austere: black line on white, two typefaces, a four-colour palette used sparingly, and large amounts of empty space. Austerity is not minimalism for its own sake — it is what keeps the one meaningful mark on a slide visible from the back of the room.

3  Accessibility specification

Target
WCAG 2.2 Level AA for all slide content, plus the Level AAA contrast criterion (1.4.6) for body text where it costs nothing — which, on white, it does not.

WCAG was written for web pages, so several criteria need translating for slides. What each one means here:

Perceivable

CriterionWhat it requires here
1.1.1 Non-text content Every slide and every animation step carries a written visual description (§10). Decorative marks — of which there should be none — would be aria-hidden.
1.3.1 Info and relationships Real headings and lists, not styled divs. Structure must survive with styling off.
1.4.1 Use of colour Colour is never the only carrier of meaning. Every colour distinction is reinforced by position, label, line style, or the description channel.
1.4.3 Contrast (minimum) 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for text ≥24px bold or ≥30px regular. All four palette colours clear 3:1 on white; three clear 4.5:1.
1.4.6 Contrast (enhanced, AAA) 7:1 for body text. Ink on white is 18.9:1 — meet it by default.
1.4.11 Non-text contrast 3:1 minimum for every diagram stroke. This is the criterion most often missed, and it is why no palette colour may be lighter than the 3:1 floor.
1.4.12 Text spacing Line height ≥1.5× for body text; paragraph spacing ≥2× font size.

Operable

CriterionWhat it requires here
2.1.1 Keyboard Every function reachable by keyboard. Advancing is keyboard-only by design.
2.2.2 Pause, stop, hide Nothing moves on its own. All motion is triggered by an explicit advance and settles within ~1.2s. No loops, no auto-advance, no ambient animation.
2.3.1 Three flashes No flashing. Trivially met, but do not introduce blink or rapid alternation.
2.3.3 Animation from interactions (AAA) Honour prefers-reduced-motion: jump to the end state, never tween.
2.4.2 / 2.4.6 Titles and headings Every section page has a descriptive <title>; every slide a heading that says what it is about.

Understandable and robust

  • 3.1.1 Language declared on every page.
  • 3.2.3 Navigation is identical on every slide and every section.
  • 4.1.2 Live regions announce step changes; controls carry accessible names and aria-pressed state.

Beyond WCAG

Three requirements WCAG does not cover but this series does, because the conditions in §1 demand them:

Required
Colour-vision safety. Every pair of simultaneously meaningful colours must remain distinguishable under protanopia, deuteranopia and tritanopia.

Greyscale survival. Every pair must differ by ≥1.8:1 in luminance, so the deck works printed in monochrome and on a failing projector.

Projection legibility. No type below 1/30 of slide height. No stroke below 1/600 of slide width.

4  Colour

Four content colours, defined once in palette.css and imported everywhere. Never retype a hex.

Colour encodes attention, not identity

A colour says how much this matters right now. It does not name a category. Do not establish "blue means RNA" — the moment a slide has no other actor, blue is simply unused and the convention forces a worse choice. A molecule may change rung when the focus moves to it; that is the system working.

Required
Start every element at ink. Promote exactly one thing to blue. Reach rung 2 only when rung 1 is occupied on that same slide. If you find yourself wanting rung 3, first check whether something already coloured should drop back to ink.

Why these four and not others

Under dichromacy there is effectively one hue axis — blue against yellow — plus lightness. Red, orange, yellow and green all collapse onto the yellow pole, so hue alone cannot separate four colours. Lightness must, and lightness is also what greyscale preserves, so one constraint solves both. The four are therefore spaced evenly in log luminance between black and the 3:1-on-white floor, giving ≥1.81:1 between every pair, not merely adjacent ones. Hue then reinforces the lightness step. Full derivation and simulations: palette.pdf.

Grounds: the same ladder applied to scope

GroundMeaningTextNote
bluea lecture openswhite10.2:1
ambera section opensblack 6.0:1. White on amber is 3.1:1 and fails — the one ground that flips.
whiteordinary contentblack18.9:1

Backgrounds are named by role in markup (data-bg="section"), never by colour, so the scheme can be repainted without touching a single slide file.

Open
Rung 3 (amber) currently has no content use, only the section ground. If a future lecture needs a genuine third simultaneous distinction, decide then whether amber is spent on it or whether the slide should be split instead. Splitting is usually the better answer.

5  Typography

Two families, no more. A slab serif carries titles; a neutral grotesque carries everything else; monospace appears only for sequence and code, where character alignment is semantic.

RoleFamilySizeNotes
Lecture titleslab84on the blue ground
Section titleslab76on the amber ground
Slide titleslab60, or 48 with a figureone line; never wrap
Body, bulletssans30 · 26 · 23 23 is the floor — 1/39 of slide height, already marginal
Sequencemono24–27letter-spaced for scanning
Captions, sourcessans18–22muted; never load-bearing
Required
Body type is 1/30 of slide height — 30 units in the 900-unit box, which is what .slide p and .slide li already set. That is the size a claim is made at, and anything the back row must read sits at it.

The deck also ships two density escapes — .tight at 26 and .tighter at 23 — and they are honest about what they are: a way to fit more on a slide than belongs there. They are legitimate for supporting bullets and reference tables. They are not legitimate for the sentence that carries the slide, and reaching for one is a signal to ask whether the slide should split instead.

Required
An annotation is never smaller than what it annotates. A label explaining a sequence block set at 26 does not sit at 24. This rule catches the common case that a flat floor misses: text that is legible in absolute terms but reads as subordinate to the thing it is supposed to explain.
  • Bold for emphasis; italic for genus, species and Latin terms. Never underline — it reads as a link and collides with descenders.
  • Sentence case throughout. No all-caps runs longer than an abbreviation.
  • Never centre body text or bullets. Centre only titles on full-bleed ground slides.
  • Maximum ~10 words per bullet, ~6 bullets per slide. Beyond that, split.

6  Layout and composition

Slides are authored in a fixed 1600×900 box scaled to fit the viewport, so all measurements are slide units and every slide is identically proportioned.

MeasureValue
Margins110 sides, 86 top, 70 bottom
Title to first content≥26
Between content blocks≥30
Figure to caption14

Whitespace is structure

Empty space is the primary grouping device — there are no boxes, rules or panels to do that job. A slide that fills its box is a slide that needs splitting. Aim for at least a third of the content area empty on a typical content slide.

Figures

Required
A figure claims the height left over after the title and text, and scales up as well as down to fill it. Cap-only sizing (max-height alone) is prohibited: it silently renders small sources at their intrinsic size.

Position artwork by its content bounding box, not its canvas. Generated art routinely carries 10–15% dead margin.

Cropping a figure to gain size is acceptable when the cropped region carries no meaning. Shrinking the title to gain figure height is not.

7  Line and diagram style

The house diagram style is open line drawing: uniform thin strokes, no fills, no shading, generous space. It reads at distance, survives greyscale, prints cleanly, and scales to any size without becoming muddy.

Stroke

UseWeight
Primary subject (the molecule under discussion)3.0
Context and secondary structure2.4–2.8
Annotation: brackets, ticks, leaders2.0–2.5
Absolute minimum2.5 (1/600 of slide width)

Round caps and joins throughout. No dashes except to mark a genuine discontinuity. No arrowheads except where an arrow means direction of a process — and never on a DNA strand, which uses the half barb below.

Fill

Fills are prohibited except in two cases: a semi-transparent enzyme envelope (fill-opacity ~0.12), and a solid mark small enough to read as a point — a terminus dot, a mutation marker. Everything else is outline.

DNA vocabulary

Required
  • Every 3′ end carries a half barb, never a full arrowhead.
  • Two molecules never abut — leave a visible nick.
  • A strand never branches from its middle. Tails leave from an end.
  • Every diagram declares polarity somehow: barbs, or 5′/3′ labels.
  • A sequence label sits on the strand it describes.
  • RNA is drawn as a wave wherever it appears as a line, so it separates from DNA by shape and label rather than by colour alone.
The one licensed exception

A borrowed figure may break these rules when breaking them is the lesson. img/dna-backbone-bond.png, the atomic backbone drawing reused across four sections, carries two full-headed grey arrows that run 3′→5′ — and one slide exists purely to ask whether they run 5′→3′, so that students discover they do not. Both arrowheads sit at 5′ ends, so no 3′ terminus is marked with a full arrow and the barb rule survives intact.

The general principle: before correcting a convention violation in inherited material, establish what the violation is doing. A rule enforced against its own purpose is worse than the inconsistency it removes.

Letters or lines?

Use letters when a position is the point — a start site, a mismatch, a recognition sequence, a cut site. Use lines when only topology matters — displacement, circularity, processivity. A line cannot show a position; letters at slide scale cost roughly 40 bases of width, so do not spend them on topology.

Enzymes

Prefer a silhouette traced from a real structure over a cartoon. Give it a visible cleft, seat that cleft on the feature it acts upon, and render it semi-transparent so the substrate reads straight through. Depict the protein that is actually doing the work — a sliding clamp is not a polymerase.

8  Motion

Motion in these decks is explanatory, never decorative. The test: if the animation were replaced by its end state, what would be lost? If the answer is "nothing", remove it.

Animate a change; never animate an arrival

Something that simply exists should appear instantly — an annealed oligo, a label, a panel. Something that happens should be tweened — synthesis proceeding, a strand peeling off, an enzyme backing up. Growing a thing into existence reads as a dissolve and implies a process that is not occurring.

Pacing

RuleValue
A mechanism getsthree states: arrival, question, outcome
Transition duration700–900ms; up to 1200ms for a composed reveal
Easingease-in-out; no bounce, no overshoot
Steps per slide≤6
Required
Every click must carry narration or perform visible work. A step with an empty note and no visual change is a step to delete.

A flourish belongs inside the transition of the click that reveals it — never as extra clicks. Flourishes play going forward only.

Reduced motion, and no ambient movement

Under prefers-reduced-motion, jump straight to the end state. Nothing ever animates without an explicit advance: no loops, no auto-play, no idle motion. This is WCAG 2.2.2, and it also means a presenter can leave a slide up indefinitely.

9  Imagery and line art

Photographs are not used. Where a scene or an object must be shown, it is line art matching the house style — generated, then vectorised to centreline curves and recoloured to the palette so it is indistinguishable from hand-drawn diagram work.

Commissioning

  • ≥2000px wide. Resolution dominates trace quality more than any other factor.
  • Uniform bold stroke, ~0.5% of image width. Drawn weight becomes final weight.
  • Outlines only — no fills, shading, hatching, stippling or grey.
  • Lines representing separate objects must not touch.
  • No text, no ghosted or duplicated elements, consistent scale between objects.

Known failure modes

InputResult
Filled regionsDestroyed — a filled arrowhead becomes a spine
Fine hatchingHundreds of junk contours; mush at slide scale
Varying stroke widthFlattened to a single median
Isolated dotsDropped as speckle

Integration

  • Traced paths live in their own module; geometry stays with the sequence that uses it.
  • Always recolour to the palette. Source colours never ship.
  • Scene art is context: rung 0. The attention colour belongs to the molecular content.
  • Multi-panel art separates by bounding-box clustering when panels do not overlap, which makes it animatable. Commission panels rather than one dense composition.

10  Text alternatives

Every step carries two independent strings. This is the WCAG 1.1.1 mechanism for the deck, and it also drives speech output and any future accessibility actuator.

<template class="notes">Spoken narration for this step.</template>
<template class="desc">What is on the screen.</template>
ChannelContainsRule
notesWhat the lecturer says Verbatim from source notes. Leave blank where the source is blank, so speech fires only on new narration.
descWhat a sighted viewer sees Describe the role, not the pigment. Update it whenever the artwork changes — it is read aloud.
Required
Write desc so that someone who cannot see the slide could reconstruct the argument from it. "Section divider: Endonucleases" — not "green slide". Hue is not information; role is.

11  Conformance checklist

Run before a deck is considered done.

#Check
1Every colour used measures ≥3:1 on white; body text ≥4.5:1.
2No distinction is carried by colour alone.
3The deck is legible with a greyscale filter applied.
4No body type below 30 units; no stroke below 2.5.
5Every slide has a heading; every step has a description.
6No step has an empty note and no visual change.
7Nothing moves without an explicit advance; reduced motion honoured.
8Every 3′ end has a half barb; no two molecules abut.
